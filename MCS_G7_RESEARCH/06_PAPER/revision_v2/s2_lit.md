# 2. Literature review and theoretical framework

*Verification note: bibliographic records were checked against publisher, repository or database records; full texts were not consulted, so statements are limited to what abstracts and records state.*

**Management control as a package.** Malmi and Brown (2008) propose a typology of planning, cybernetic, reward and compensation, administrative and cultural controls and call for research on controls as a package. Grabner and Moers (2013) distinguish packages from designed systems and define internal consistency. The observed presence of a practice is not evidence that it is integrated into a coherent system. WBES covers only selected cybernetic (monitoring, targets) and reward (bonus) controls; it does not measure the package.

**Use of controls.** Simons (1995) distinguishes belief, boundary, diagnostic and interactive systems by the way managers use them; Tessier and Otley (2012) show conceptual ambiguity and propose separating managerial intentions from employee perceptions. Because WBES does not measure diagnostic or interactive use, Simons is used here only to formulate propositions.

**Management practices and performance.** Bloom and Van Reenen (2007) relate scored practices to productivity, profitability and survival; Bloom et al. (2019) document large dispersion in US plants, and Scur et al. (2021) review the World Management Survey. Bloom, Sadun and Van Reenen (2012) report that decentralisation in firms varies with country-level trust, an example of context shaping organisational choice. A universalist reading predicts a stable positive association between practice sophistication and productivity; we distinguish the accumulation of practices, their implementation, their integration and their contextual effectiveness.

**Contingency and institutional perspectives.** Contingency research asks how control design relates to context (Chenhall, 2003); Otley (2016) argues that a mechanistic, predictive search for optimal control systems is a dead end and that studies should follow changing packages and the mechanisms deployed. Drazin and Van de Ven (1985) distinguish selection, interaction and systems forms of fit, and Gresov and Drazin (1997) distinguish forms of equifinality. Institutional perspectives emphasise rules and routines in management accounting change (Burns & Scapens, 2000), isomorphic pressures (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) and national institutional arrangements (Hall & Soskice, 2001). Cross-cultural work on management control is reviewed by Harrison and McKinnon (1999), while McSweeney (2002) criticises treating national cultures as causal entities. We therefore treat culture, institutions and country fixed effects as different concepts: the data contain only country identifiers, which cannot be interpreted as measures of either culture or institutions.

**Exploratory methods.** Nielsen (2022) argues that exploratory data analysis and unsupervised learning should be part of management accounting research. We use latent class and continuous latent-variable models as complementary lenses on heterogeneity; statistical structure is not theoretical interpretation, and neither method guarantees that the structure it finds corresponds to meaningful organisational systems. Configurational work on control (Bedford & Malmi, 2015) and latent class analysis of Enterprise Surveys data on transactional governance (Murrell, Karalashvili & Francis, 2024) are the closest precedents.

**Framework.** Figure 1 links management practices, organisational implementation, contextual alignment and outcomes. Practices, their statistical structure and outcomes are observed or estimable; implementation (use, articulation, managerial ownership) and contextual alignment are not observed and are treated as theoretical. International diffusion of controls — budgets, indicators, targets, incentives — may fail to produce integrated decision-making because of limited managerial ownership, misalignment with decision processes, weak linkage with routines, incompatible incentives, institutional constraints, insufficient capabilities or resistance to change. These are theoretical mechanisms; WBES cannot show why a particular organisation abandoned a control reform.
