# 6. Results

**6.1 Sample and descriptive differences (retained).** The module universe contains 6,316 establishments (Table 1). Reported use of indicator monitoring (r2) ranges from 47% in Japan to 92% in the United Kingdom, targets (r4) from 60% in Italy to 93% in the United Kingdom, and target-linked bonuses (r8) from 36% in Italy to 76% in the United Kingdom. Larger firms report more practices (mean core score 1.86 for firms with fewer than 50 employees and 2.51 for 250 or more). These differences are descriptive and confounded by size composition; they are not evidence of institutional or cultural effects.

**6.2 Structure of observed practices (retained).** Latent class models fit the six common items poorly as distinct types (Table 2): BIC is lowest at four classes but differs by at most 13 points between three and five classes, relative entropy is 0.53–0.60, and residual dependence between items is large. The three-class solution is ordered along the core score without crossings on monitoring, targets and bonuses (0 of 9 comparisons; the ordering is partly by construction because classes are ordered by the same items). On the same likelihood, a hybrid model (three classes × one trait) has a BIC 129 points below the best latent class model, and two-dimensional continuous models outperform latent class models by 236 to 368 points in the specifications that include conditional items. Parallel analysis retains one factor (eigenvalue 2.54 versus 1.09). The reliability of the one-dimensional trait is 0.60 (Figure 2). A small class in the four-class solution (about 5–7%) pairs near-universal targets with few bonuses but is unstable across subsamples and weights. Conclusion retained: for these six items, a common continuous dimension is a better description than distinct configurations; this does not refute the package view, because most control categories are unobserved.

**6.3 Measurement comparability (extended).** Multi-group results depend on the number of classes (Table 3). With three classes full invariance is rejected by BIC (free item parameters preferred by 245 points), with differential functioning for r2, r8, r10 and r11; with four classes the invariant model is preferred by 541 points and only r8 shows differential functioning. Country-specific latent class models favour two classes in Canada, France, Germany, Japan and the United States and three in Italy and the United Kingdom, and one-factor solutions are similar across countries (congruence 0.93–0.99). Under country-specific priors, the reliability of the trait score varies markedly: Canada 0.64, France 0.40, Germany 0.57, Italy 0.65, Japan 0.35, United Kingdom 0.42, United States 0.57. Cross-country comparisons of slopes are therefore qualified by partial invariance and differential reliability.

**6.4 Country-specific associations with performance (new).** Table 4 and Figure 3 report country-specific slopes of the calibrated latent trait. For productivity, slopes are positive with confidence intervals excluding zero in Canada, Germany, Italy, Japan and the United States, and close to zero with wide intervals in France (0.033) and the United Kingdom (0.037). Cochran's Q is 17.7 (df = 6, p = 0.007, I² = 66%), the random-effects mean is 0.169 and the 95% prediction interval, −0.007 to 0.345, includes zero. For sales and employment growth, individual slopes are small and mostly consistent with zero, and heterogeneity is not significant (Q p = 0.081 and 0.110). Formal pooled tests (Table 5) reject equality of productivity slopes in 12 of 16 specifications (p-values range from below 0.001 to 0.126); equality is not rejected when the pooled-prior EAP is used without country-specific control slopes (p = 0.12) or with weights (Q p = 0.84). The evidence for cross-country heterogeneity in the productivity association is therefore moderate and specification-dependent. The two countries with weak slopes are also those with the highest reported adoption of monitoring (85% and 92%) and targets (87% and 93%) and with low calibrated reliability (0.40 and 0.42); across the seven countries the calibrated slope is negatively related to the country mean of the trait (Spearman ρ = −0.75, p = 0.052; n = 7, descriptive). This pattern is compatible with ceiling effects, restricted range or measurement error and cannot be attributed to institutions or culture.

**6.5 Organisational contingencies (new).** Among the six firm-level moderators of the productivity association (Table 6, Figure 4), only foreign ownership shows a sizeable interaction (0.120 log points per SD of the score; 95% CI 0.020 to 0.220; unadjusted p = 0.019), and it does not survive Holm correction (p = 0.33; 0.137 with p = 0.008 and Holm p = 0.14 when country-specific slopes are included). Size, age, export status and manager characteristics show no reliable moderation, and no moderator is significant for either growth outcome after correction. The labels of the ownership, export and manager variables are unverified for the G7 files.

**6.6 Configurations and equifinality (new).** Latent classes add no explanatory power beyond country-specific trait slopes for any outcome (p = 0.78, 0.18, 0.51). Class-by-country interactions are not significant for productivity (p = 0.33) but are for sales growth (F(12) = 2.45, Holm p = 0.021, ΔR² = 0.004) and employment growth (F(12) = 2.29, Holm p = 0.033, ΔR² = 0.004); the increments are tiny and may reflect country-specific meaning of pooled classes given partial invariance. Among firms with the same core score, combinations of practices differ in productivity at level two (Wald p = 0.009, Holm p = 0.26; Table 7), mainly because firms with monitoring and bonuses but no targets (101) have higher productivity than those with monitoring and targets but no bonuses (110) (difference 0.187, 95% CI 0.067 to 0.308). Equivalence within ±0.05 log points is not established for any contrast because confidence intervals are wide, so the data neither demonstrate nor reject equifinality. Country-level tests were possible only in Canada, Germany, Italy and the United States (minimum cell size 15) and none is significant after correction.

**6.7 Nonlinearity (new for countries; pooled result retained).** Pooled, a spline is preferred to a linear term for productivity (p = 0.006), but the largest departure from linearity is 0.03 standard deviations of the outcome. Across seven countries and three outcomes, no spline-versus-linear test survives Holm correction and the largest deviations are at most 0.13 outcome standard deviations. Nonlinearity is not a substantive finding.

**6.8 Level dependence and common support (new).** Because the two countries with weak productivity slopes also report the highest adoption, two diagnostics were run (pooled score on a common scale). On the range of the score present in all countries (−0.40 to 0.69 pooled SD; 29–51% of each country's firms), heterogeneity in productivity slopes is attenuated (Q = 11.9, p = 0.064, I² = 50%) but not removed, and country slopes become imprecise. Pooled within countries, the productivity slope is 0.164 (95% CI 0.112 to 0.216) in the lower third of the score, 0.139 (0.068 to 0.211) in the middle third and 0.013 (−0.094 to 0.121) in the upper third; equality of the lower and upper slopes is rejected (p = 0.009, unadjusted). Allowing this common level-dependent shape lowers the Wald statistic for country-specific productivity slopes from 10.1 (p = 0.122) to 8.0 (p = 0.241); it makes no difference for growth outcomes. Within countries, slopes below and above the country median differ significantly only in Canada (−0.52, p = 0.002; seven tests, not adjusted). Part of the cross-country difference in productivity slopes is therefore compatible with a common level-dependent association; the remainder is unexplained.

**6.9 Associations with performance (retained).** Pooled within countries, one standard deviation of the pooled-prior score is associated with 0.128 log points higher sales per employee (about 13.7%), 0.0033 higher annual sales growth and 0.0018 higher annual employment growth; classes add no predictive information once the continuous score is included (pseudo-class tests p = 0.29–0.62), and out-of-sample gains are modest for productivity (cross-validated RMSE 0.810 to 0.803) and negligible for growth. These are associations, not effects.
