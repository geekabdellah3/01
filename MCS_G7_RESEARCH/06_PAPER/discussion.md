# 5. Discussion

**What can the co-occurrence of management practices tell us about management control systems?** Our central empirical result is negative with respect to configurations: in these data, the observable practices share a dominant continuous dimension; latent classes are poorly separated, are ordered along that dimension when K = 3, and add no explanatory value over a continuous score. That firms with more practices obtain higher scores is unsurprising. What the analysis adds is a test of a prior question: whether the structure of observed practices *warrants* a configurational reading at all. Within the six universal WBES items, it largely does not.

**Three concepts that co-occurrence data cannot separate.** We distinguish (i) *accumulation*, the number of monitoring, target and incentive practices a firm has; (ii) *configuration*, combinations whose character is not reducible to that number; and (iii) *integration*, practices that are articulated and used jointly to steer behaviour and decisions. Our data speak, in a limited way, to (i) and (ii): accumulation accounts for most detectable structure, and configuration adds little. They are silent on (iii). A firm may hold dashboards, targets and bonuses without these operating as a coherent system, which is the package-versus-system distinction of Grabner and Moers (2013). The implication, offered as a **theoretical interpretation and not as a demonstrated result**, is that inferring a control *system* from the observable presence of practices is hazardous, and that future work should measure articulation and use directly.

**Relation to the three frameworks.** Our results do not refute Malmi and Brown (2008): WBES observes mainly cybernetic and reward controls, so a package perspective covering cultural, planning and administrative controls is not tested. Relative to Bloom and Van Reenen (2007), an aggregate score is not shown to be inadequate for the outcomes examined. Relative to Simons (1995), monitoring and targets are compatible with diagnostic control, but nothing in WBES identifies interactive use.

**A tentative lead.** The four-class solution contains a small class (about 5–7%) with near-universal targets but few bonuses. One could conjecture that targets without matched incentives reflect incomplete integration, but the class is unstable across subsamples and weights and we draw no conclusion from it.

**Significance, economic importance, prediction and causality.** One standard deviation of the continuous score is associated with about 13.7% higher sales per employee within country (exp(0.128) − 1), a statistically precise and economically non-trivial association, but the out-of-sample gain is modest (cross-validated RMSE 0.810 to 0.803, about 0.9%). For annual sales growth (+0.33 percentage points per SD) and employment growth (+0.18 points), the predictive gain is negligible (about 0.1% and 0%). None of these is evidence that accumulating practices causes better performance: the data are cross-sectional and self-reported, productivity may be partly reversed in causality, and unobserved firm quality may drive both.

The results are mainly descriptive and should not be treated as a theory of management control.
