## 2.1 Structured management practices as the measurable starting point
Bloom and Van Reenen (2007) measure practices of monitoring, target setting and incentives and relate them to productivity; later work reviews why practices differ across firms and countries (Bloom & Van Reenen, 2010) and asks whether management is better seen as a technology than as a contingent style (Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, 2016). The universalist reading predicts a common association between practices and performance, with countries differing mainly in adoption. This is the benchmark against which the present article defines heterogeneity.

## 2.2 Bridge to management control
Monitoring corresponds to cybernetic and diagnostic control (measuring and tracking results), targets to planning and results control (setting objectives and orienting behaviour), and incentives to reward control (aligning effort and rewards); the categories follow Malmi and Brown (2008) and, for results controls, Merchant and Van der Stede. Ferreira and Otley (2009) and Otley (1999) describe performance management systems through areas of targets, rewards and information flows that these practices also cover. The correspondence is interpretive. The three practices are not a complete control system: belief and boundary systems, interactive use, administrative and cultural controls, and internal consistency are not observed, so the study concerns *observable management control practices*.

## 2.3 Why combinations matter
Malmi and Brown (2008) argue that controls should be studied as a package, and Grabner and Moers (2013) distinguish a package from a system with internal consistency. Simons (1995) and Tessier and Otley (2012) show that effects depend on how controls are used. Combinations may therefore matter beyond individual practices: a firm that tracks indicators without targets differs from one that also links them to targets and rewards. Complementarity in the sense of supermodularity (Milgrom & Roberts, 1995) is stronger than a statistical interaction, and cross-sectional tests of complementarity can be biased (Athey & Stern, 1998). Interactions among practices are thus treated as statistical interaction, not as evidence of integration.

## 2.4 Contingency
Contingency theory holds that performance depends on fit between practices and conditions (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Donaldson, 2001); forms of fit differ (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985), and the contingency literature on control is reviewed by Chenhall (2003) and Otley (2016), who doubts that a predictive search for optimal systems can succeed. Organisational contingencies observed in WBES are size, age, ownership, exports and manager characteristics.

## 2.5 Institutional theory and transferability
Formal practices may be adopted for legitimacy and decoupled from operations (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Zbaracki, 1998), isomorphic pressures make organisations similar (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), and national institutional arrangements differ (Hall & Soskice, 2001). Transfer success depends on institutionalisation at the receiving unit, the institutional profile of the country and organisational context (Kostova, 1999), and adoption can be ceremonial (Kostova & Roth, 2002). Culture, institutions and country fixed effects are different things: McSweeney (2002) criticises treating national cultures as causal entities, and Harrison and McKinnon (1999) review the difficulties of cross-cultural control research. The data contain only country identifiers, which are not measures of culture or institutions.

## 2.6 Exploratory methods
Nielsen (2022) argues for exploratory data analysis and unsupervised learning in management accounting. Latent class and continuous latent-variable models are used here only as complementary analyses of the structure of the items; statistical structure is not theoretical interpretation.
