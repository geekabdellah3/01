## 1. Phase 1 — The five subquestions (answers from the evidence)

**Q1. When do IPC/IPR changes generate a need for adaptation?** No study tests this as a gap. Convergent signals of strain exist:
- A2: joint use of automation and analytics lowers effectiveness (b = −0.222, p < 0.01; "might be a result of resource constraints").
- A27: higher data volume is negatively associated with counter-measure effectiveness.
- A29: KPI overload leaves analysts overwhelmed and the process under-controlled.
- A34: data collection and reliability crowd out advice, most at intermediate maturity.

Adaptation is **not automatic**:
- A7: no significant effect of digitalisation on the organisation of MC or on strategic instruments.
- A37: the centralisation path is unsupported (β = 0.113, p = 0.083; indirect 0.035, p = 0.391).
- A40: structures and resistance limit use.
- A26: digitalisation alone has no direct performance effect.

**Q2. How can sensing/seizing/transforming help?** Only A12 measures these, and as antecedents of digital transformation success. Everything else is an activity consistent with the triad (see §4).

**Q3. Which MCS components are actually transformed?** Planning and cybernetic controls carry the evidence. Administrative evidence is mixed. Cultural evidence is qualitative. **Reward has one framework-only row (A48).**

**Q4. Interdependencies?** Thin: A37, A28, A40, A1, A12/A39. No study documents coordinated cross-control change.

**Q5. Recursion?** Not demonstrated. There are fragments (A29's BI → alarm-overload → AI sequence, which is a designed artefact; A48/A49/A18 longitudinal or iterative designs). There is no traced endogenous loop.

## 9. Revised Section 4.5 (draft)

*Citations use `[A#]` where the matrix gives no authors; replace with author-year. Names are given where the matrix supplies them. ≈ 700 words.*

> **4.5. Dynamic Capabilities and the Reconfiguration of Management Control Packages**
>
> **Informational tensions and the case for adaptation.** Greater analytical capacity does not by itself improve control. Digitalisation shows no direct performance effect when planning and budgeting performance is held constant [A26], joint use of automation and analytics is associated with lower finance-function effectiveness [A2], and larger data volumes are associated with poorer counter-measure effectiveness in forecasting [A27]. Case evidence points the same way: alarm overload in KPI monitoring [A29] and data-reliability work that displaces advisory work [A34]. These findings are consistent with information-processing requirements growing alongside capacity, although none of the studies measures capacity or requirements directly. Adaptation is also not automatic. Digitalisation shows no significant effect on the organisation of management control or on strategic instruments [A7], does not shift the locus of information or decision centralisation [Gimpl et al., 2026, A37], and in some firms is absorbed without enhancing the controller's contribution [A40].
>
> **Sensing, seizing and transforming.** Following Teece (2007), we treat these as organisation-level capabilities, not as properties of technologies or roles. Only one study measures them, and it relates interactive control use and belief systems to digital dynamic capabilities and, in turn, to transformation success (β = 0.355, 0.279 and 0.789) [A12]. The remaining evidence consists of activities that can be read in these terms: recognising epistemic tension [A1] or compliance demands [Getzin et al., 2026, A45]; piloting, phasing and mobilising resources [A9, A17]; and changes in KPI practice, performance-measurement systems and organisation [A28, A49, A9]. These readings are our interpretation. We find no study showing that a capability explains a particular control reconfiguration, and the one measured link runs from controls to capabilities, not the reverse.
>
> **Reconfiguration within and across the package.** Using Malmi and Brown's (2008) categories, the evidence is uneven. Planning and cybernetic controls are best documented [A26, A27, A28, A49]. Evidence for administrative controls is mixed, combining qualitative change [A9, A45] with null quantitative results [A7, A37]. Cultural controls appear mainly as persisting tensions between epistemic cultures [A1] and as conditions affecting use [A40]. For reward controls, the only relevant item is a design framework without reported outcomes [A48]. Relationships between controls are rarely traced. Two studies indicate that a change in performance measurement alters managers' responsibilities [A28] and that digitalisation operates through standardisation to information quality [A37]. Others show that structure and acceptance condition the effect of technology [A40]. We found no study documenting coordinated change across several controls, and we do not claim that all five categories are reconfigured.
>
> **Asymmetric and recursive reconfiguration.** The evidence is consistent with partial and uneven adaptation: tensions persist in some cases [A1, A40], and local practice must be preserved for a common platform to work [A28]. Whether adaptation produces new requirements that prompt further adaptation is, in the corpus, suggested but not demonstrated. One design study moved from a BI alarm overload to an AI filter [A29], and longitudinal cases show continuing change [A18, A49], but cross-sectional associations cannot support a feedback loop. We therefore advance two propositions for testing. *P1:* an increase in information-processing capacity that is not matched by changes in the controls governing interpretation and responsibility is associated with weaker perceived effectiveness. *P2:* a change in one control that alters what must be measured, interpreted or accounted for is followed by changes in at least one other control. Both could be falsified by longitudinal cases that trace sequences of changes, and by comparison of firms with and without such changes.
>
> This raises the question of when reconfiguration restores informational fit, when it leaves partial fit, and when misfit persists, which the next section examines.
