# 6. Results

**6.1 Sample (Table 1).** The module universe contains 6,316 establishments. Reported use of monitoring ranges from 47% in Japan to 92% in the United Kingdom, targets from 60% in Italy to 93% in the United Kingdom, and target-linked bonuses from 36% in Italy to 76% in the United Kingdom. These descriptive differences are confounded by size composition and are not evidence of institutional or cultural effects.

**6.2 Individual associations (Table 2).** With the three practices entered together, incentives show the strongest association with productivity (0.199 log points, 95% CI 0.156 to 0.243, about 22% higher sales per employee), followed by monitoring (0.122; 0.069 to 0.175; about 13%); targets are weakly associated and not distinguishable from zero (0.047; −0.010 to 0.104). For sales growth, targets (0.0077) and incentives (0.0064) are positively associated and monitoring is not; for employment growth only incentives are (0.0079; 0.0044 to 0.0113). Using the breadth of monitoring (three bands of r3) instead of its presence, each band is associated with 0.085 log points of productivity (0.063 to 0.107), and the association of targets falls to 0.019. These are conditional associations within sector-by-country cells; they do not show that adopting a practice raises performance.

**6.3 Combinations (Table 3).** No pairwise interaction is distinguishable from zero for productivity or sales growth, and the joint pairwise test is not significant for any outcome (p = 0.35, 0.94, 0.14). The three-way interaction is positive for productivity (0.229; 0.010 to 0.448; p = 0.040) but is absent for the growth outcomes (p = 0.99, 0.84) and does not survive correction for the three outcomes tested; an earlier analysis of combinations at equal numbers of practices likewise found no robust difference. The data therefore show no evidence that the practices operate jointly in a way that goes beyond their individual associations.

**6.4 Stability across countries (Tables 4 and 5, Figure 2).** For productivity, the association of incentives varies strongly across countries: 0.509 in Canada (0.333 to 0.684), 0.360 in Italy, 0.225 in Germany, 0.202 in the United Kingdom, 0.154 in the United States, 0.082 in Japan (−0.006 to 0.171) and 0.048 in France (−0.079 to 0.175); Cochran's Q = 28.4 (df = 6, p = 0.0001; Holm-adjusted 0.0009), I² = 79%. The association of monitoring is positive in the United States (0.193), Japan (0.146) and Germany (0.089) and not distinguishable from zero elsewhere, with no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.39); targets show no association with productivity in any country (p = 0.63 for equality). Descriptively, the productivity association is concentrated in incentives in Canada and Italy and is of similar size for monitoring and incentives in the United States and Japan, but differences between practice coefficients within a country were not tested. For growth, the association of targets with sales growth differs across countries (p = 0.0027; Holm 0.021); other practice-by-outcome tests are not significant after Holm correction when HC3 standard errors are used, while sector-clustered standard errors yield more rejections (Table 5). Heterogeneity is therefore practice-specific: it is strong for incentives and productivity, and weak or absent for monitoring and targets.

**6.5 Organisational contingencies (Table 6).** Among the six organisational moderators, only size shows a joint interaction with the practices for productivity (Wald χ² = 11.5, p = 0.009), which does not survive correction over the 18 tests (Holm p = 0.17); foreign ownership (p = 0.048; Holm 0.71) and the other moderators show no reliable moderation, and none is significant for growth after correction.

**6.6 Out-of-sample comparison (Table 7).** For productivity, common slopes for the three practices (P1) lower out-of-sample RMSE from 0.7924 to 0.7854, a 1.8% reduction in mean squared error (corrected 95% CI 0.0055 to 0.0167 in squared log points; p < 0.001). Adding combinations (−0.0002), organisational contingency (−0.0010), country-specific slopes for each practice (0.0002; corrected CI −0.0031 to 0.0035; p = 0.91) or both brings no reliable gain. For growth outcomes no model improves on the controls by more than 0.25%. The common model is therefore not improved on out of sample, even for the practice, incentives, whose association varies most across countries.

**6.7 Why does a common model remain competitive?** The statistically detectable heterogeneity is small relative to what out-of-sample prediction can resolve. If country-specific slopes were known exactly, the mean squared error would fall by 0.0028 (0.45% of the residual variance), almost entirely through incentives (heterogeneity standard deviation τ = 0.114), whereas estimating eighteen additional slopes costs about 0.0021 out of sample; the expected net gain is 0.0008. In simulations at the estimated heterogeneity, national slopes beat the common model in only 53% of replications (mean gain 0.0007); at twice the estimated heterogeneity they do in 95%. The competitiveness of the common model thus reflects the small share of variance that practices explain together with the cost of estimating country-specific slopes; it does not show that associations are homogeneous.

**6.8 Transferability (Table 8).** Out-of-sample R² for productivity is small in every country (local models: −0.02 to 0.08). A model estimated in a single country is worse than the local model in 93% of the 42 source–target pairs, by 0.033 R² units on average, with large losses for some pairs (Canada and Italy as sources for France: −0.118 and −0.111); this is a larger loss than for a single global score of practices, consistent with a score masking differences between practices. A model estimated on the other six countries (LOCO) performs close to the local model: local minus LOCO is 0.001 to 0.022 in six countries and −0.0005 in France, with 95% intervals that include zero in every country (for the United States 0.019, −0.000 to 0.035); adding the target's own training folds to the other countries changes little. Growth outcomes show no transferable signal.

**6.9 Complementary analyses of latent structure (retained from the earlier analysis).** For the six items asked of all firms (r1, r2, r4, r8, r10, r11), the items follow one dominant continuous dimension (parallel analysis: eigenvalue 2.54 versus 1.09), latent classes are poorly separated (relative entropy 0.53 to 0.60), and continuous or hybrid models fit better than purely discrete models (BIC differences of 129 to 368 points); no framework-implied split of seven items with verified or partly verified wording (cybernetic versus reward; monitoring versus targets versus rewards) improves on a single factor (BIC +8.5 to +43.4). Cross-country invariance is partial and depends on the number of classes, and the reliability of the latent score varies across countries from 0.35 to 0.65 depending on the prior used. With the latent score, productivity slopes are positive in most countries, heterogeneous in most specifications and flatten at high levels of the score (0.164 in the lower third, 0.013 in the upper third); allowing a common level-dependent shape reduces cross-country heterogeneity, so part of it may reflect adoption level, ceiling or measurement error. A sensitivity analysis that excludes r10 and r11 (seven items with verified or partly verified wording: r1, r2, r3, r4, r5, r8, r9) gives the same conclusions: a common latent slope lowers out-of-sample mean squared error by 1.4% (corrected 95% CI 0.0044 to 0.0137), contingency, country-specific slopes and level-dependent shapes bring no reliable gain (corrected p of at least 0.39), and the reliability of this score by country is 0.47 to 0.67 with a pooled prior. These analyses support the use of the three practice indicators as separate variables: a single score masks the practice-specific heterogeneity found in Section 6.4.
