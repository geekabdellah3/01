                  Introduction
                  In literature, the role that firms play in the activities of innovation has been emphasized
                  for many years. A variety of theories, discussions, and disagreements have been made
                  (Atoko & Tlangelani, 2024). Firms are thought of as the catalyst for economic growth
                  and as a gear-shifter for transforming the economy. They are essential to the operation of
                  the market through resource allocation, production, and creation of job opportunities.
                  Government policies, institutions, markets, and other systems all play a part in coordi-
                  nating these transactions. Numerous theories and frameworks have been developed to
                  explain how firms make decisions and behave to maximize profits (Foss, 1998). The crea-
                  tion of new technologies and techniques targeted at raising productivity will reflect this.
                  Economies would grow more slowly without this innovation and use of capital. Manu-
                  facturing firms in this regard play pivotal roles and generate multiple effects on the other
                  economic sectors and lead to economic transformation in a non-linear pattern. This
                  dynamic process continues until manufacturing takes the lead position and contributes
                  to GDP (Chenery & Taylor, 1968; Kongsamut et al., 1997; Kuznets, 1966).
                    Innovation has recently been acknowledged as a key component of the green indus-
                  trial revolution that determines productivity growth and sustainability. It is audaciously
                  included in the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG), which acknowledges its signifi-
                  cance and ability to enable research and development efforts that achieve all other SDGs
                  (Fendoğlu & Polat, 2021). It is also believed that innovation creates a significant, per-
                  vasive, and long-lasting disparity in business productivity even in developed countries
                  (Foster et al., 2001; Jorgenson, 2011; Syverson, 2011). For instance, extensive innovation
                  was a major factor in the rapid rise of Asian economies in the 1970s and 1980s by secur-
                  ing durable and sustainable productivity growth (Bloom et al., 2019; Fagerberg & Verspa-
                  gen, 2002; Fagerberg et al., 2010). As a result of this, consumers benefit from innovation
                  by receiving lower prices and higher-quality items, diverse goods and services (Fager-
                  berg, 2006; Simanis & Hart, 2011). However, the empirical evidence revealed that the
                  nexus between innovation, productivity, and employment is complicated (Edquist et al.,
                  2001; Harrison et al., 2014).
                    African governments in this regard have tried to implement several reforms and recon-
                  sider their industrialization policies. In addition, they provide certain industries with
                  extra oversight and support, expand access to development resources, and enforce laws
                  requiring joint ventures with international companies. The incentive packages also con-
                  tain cash incentives, income tax, and customs. The objective of these packages, despite
                  their varying techniques and conditions, is to assist businesses and facilitate the nation’s
                  industrialization. (EIC, 2020). Since the year 2000, the continent’s economic growth has
                  been steadily dropping, going from a peak of 6.6% in 2002 to − 2% in 2020 (WB, 2019).
                  When research and innovation data are considered, the situation becomes worse. Africa
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                         Page 3 of 53




                  generates 0.1% of all patents, 1.3% of research spending, and barely 2% of global research
                  output (Simpkin et al., 2019). In all areas of innovation, the participation rate is only 10%.
                  Only 0.1 percent of GDP is spent on research and development (UNESCO, 2018). These
                  put a daunting challenge on labor productivity and employment opportunities. There-
                  fore, the study raises the following research questions: (1) what makes up productivity,
                  employment, and innovation at the firm level in Africa? (2) Do different types of firm-
                  level innovation affect different labor groups and their productivities in the same ways?
                  and (3) Do the effects of innovation intensity and selection differ from one another?
                    Despite the significance of this issue, only a small number of studies, meanwhile,
                  looked empirically into the relationship between employment, productivity, and inno-
                  vation (Getaw et al., 2022). Based on statistics from Africa, a more thorough examina-
                  tion of the intricate and complex relationship between innovation, employment, and
                  production is required. Robust analysis techniques such as dose–response modeling and
                  propensity score matching was not used in most previous studies to ensure robustness.
                  Additionally, they were unable to fully capture the dynamic of innovation by looking at
                  various labor segments and kinds of invention as well as by including lag time in the
                  model specification. This study’s contribution is to close these research gaps. The issue
                  of discovering a novel approach to capturing the intersection of firm-level innovation,
                  employment, and productivity is important to industrial economics and labor markets,
                  which underlines the significance of this research in the African context.
                    The primary objective of this study is to assess how various forms of firm-level inno-
                  vation influence both productivity and employment in different segments of the labor
                  force (e.g., skilled, unskilled, production, and non-production workers). Additionally,
                  the study aims to determine the extent to which innovation intensity—measured by the
                  “dose” of innovation—affects these labor outcomes using pseudo-panel data from the
                  World Bank Industry Survey.
                    By studying the effects of firm-level innovation on labor market dynamics and produc-
                  tivity in Africa, this study adds to the corpus of knowledge on the subject. The study’s
                  potential to assist governments, international organizations, and business communi-
                  ties in developing policies and strategies that support various forms of innovation and
                  labor segments accounts for its practical value. These findings are essential for ensuring
                  productivity and employment-creation benefits of innovation. By doing this, the study’s
                  originality is upheld and demonstrated as rigorous scientific research is carried out; all
                  sources and support obtained throughout the creation of this work have been acknowl-
                  edged; the research gaps are filled; and certain values are added in the context of Africa.


                  Review of related literature
                  Theoretical literature review
                  The theory of innovation is traced back to the first work of Joseph Schumpeter dur-
                  ing the pre-1950 period. He was also the first to use the term “creative destruction” to
                  describe how the old is being constantly replaced by the new (new technologies, new
                  kinds of products, new methods of production, and new means of distribution) and
                  identified the most prominent and linear stages of innovation (Schumpeter, 1934). Later
                  on, the two versions of the linear model of innovation (technology push and market pull)
                  emerged in the 1950s and up to the early 1970s. However, they were criticized as they
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                      Page 4 of 53




                  gave more emphasis on incremental change in innovation and neglected a range of inno-
                  vation dimensions including its complex nature (Foxon, 2003; Nemet, 2007; Philip et al.,
                  2012). Induced innovation, evolutionary approaches, and path-dependent models then
                  emerged in the 1970s to 1990s (Ruttan, 2001). The paradigm that was shifted to reflect
                  the complexity and interdependency of the innovation process over the period 1980s to
                  the 2000s is known as the innovation system as designed by the Oslo Manual (OECD,
                  2005). Thus, following the World Bank Enterprise Survey, this study categorized innova-
                  tion into three: (1) product innovation comprises adopting new or improved goods or
                  services, introducing them to the market, and developing new or better ways to manu-
                  facture goods or render services; (2) process innovation focusses on developing new or
                  better organizational structures, new or better marketing strategies, new or better man-
                  agement practices, and new or better logistics, delivery, or distribution systems; (3) R&D
                  innovation looks at employee time spent developing new ideas, and R&D expenditures.
                    Both innovation and productivity are closely intertwined in the firms’ optimization
                  process through an effective way of doing business, lowering the cost of production,
                  optimizing processes, and providing a guarantee for market relevance. Utilizing inno-
                  vation-driven scale economies increases the production of goods and services relative to
                  inputs, leading to a positive relationship between innovation and productivity. The other
                  explanation indicates that innovation enables firms to exhaustively run unused capacity
                  utilization, and thus increases outputs without hiring additional inputs. On top of this,
                  innovations have led to new methods of creating previously known goods that require
                  less input, and thus the production frontier has shifted outward, enhancing efficiency.
                  As a result, firms can boost output, which raises overall productivity in general (Farrell,
                  1957; Pierre & Hall, 2013).
                    Specific to product innovation, there are two scenarios depending on whether the
                  newly introduced products replace the old or it added to an established business. As the
                  new products replace the old products and push them out of the market, the revenues
                  from producing the old products may be cannibalized since they require fewer inputs
                  to produce than the old products. So, a new product when introduced to the market,
                  generates new demand, which can lead to scale economies in its production or increased
                  productivity. In the case of adding a new product, the opposite may occur. Economies of
                  scale of the new products concurrently with the old ones. The firm possibly progresses
                  through the learning curve, productivity initially drops and then increases later on.
                    On the other hand, process innovation allows new processes to remove more
                  expensive factors of production, which lowers production costs, and enhances pro-
                  ductivity. Additionally, R&D innovation supports firms’ absorptive capacity (ability
                  to identify, assimilate, transform, and use external knowledge), which is necessary
                  for learning by doing. Firms that engage in internal research and development have
                  access to external knowledge, promote spillovers, and increase productivity (Cohen
                  & Levinthal, 1989; Griffith et al., 2004; Pierre & Hall, 2013). Besides, it has also an
                  indirect effect on the reaction of the market to innovation. For instance, a productiv-
                  ity-driven price decrease, and if demand is price-responsive enough, leads to a more
                  than proportional increase in sales and this increase in sales can then spur additional
                  productivity increases. The degree of market rivalry, which can be a consequence of
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                  Page 5 of 53




                  the innovation’s relevance, impacts how much the unit cost reductions translate into a
                  price decrease (Pierre & Hall, 2013).
                     The introduction of new or significantly enhanced products should increase demand
                  for innovative firms and, consequently, employment levels. Nonetheless, innovative
                  firms that have a temporary period of market dominance could establish pricing that
                  maximizes profits and cut production to the point where the net effect on employ-
                  ment following the substitution of the new offering is negative. However, even though
                  process innovation usually results in labor savings, its impact on employment is not
                  always clear-cut. If fewer people are needed to produce the same amount of labor, the
                  company can pass this efficiency advantage on to customers in the form of lower pric-
                  ing, which will boost demand. Compensation mechanisms can offset the labor-saving
                  impact of process innovation, depending on market structure, demand elasticity, and
                  the elasticity of labor-capital substitution (Spiezia & Vivarelli, 2002).
                     The intricate link between employment and innovation hinges on whether innova-
                  tion creates or destroys jobs, and could be the main source of employment dynamics
                  (Blanchflower & Burgess, 1996). The impact of innovation has also quality and quan-
                  tity aspects. The quality aspect of the impact examines the type of employment that
                  innovation either creates or destroys, leading to changes in skill changes and wage
                  structure. While the immediate effects of research expenditures that result in process
                  innovation are typically labor-saving, spending on R&D that leads to product inno-
                  vation is typically labor-friendly (Vivarelli, 2015). Product innovation may imply the
                  emergence of new firms and new sectors and thus new jobs. The main point of con-
                  tention in the present product innovation debate is whether or not innovation entails
                  job creation. The impact of product innovation on labor-friendly conditions is indis-
                  putable, yet it might vary depending on the situation. The “substitution effect”, which
                  refers to the replacement of mature products with new ones, must be weighed against
                  the “welfare effect” of product innovation, which is the development of new goods
                  (Katsoulacos, 1986).
                     Process innovation leads directly to labor-saving (job-destroying) effects. This is
                  primarily because of the introduction of labor-saving innovation that enables the
                  production of the same amount of output with a reduced workforce. Through the
                  introduction of new goods and markets, product innovation may have the impact of
                  generating jobs. However, even in the case of process innovation, market processes
                  ultimately determine the influence on labor demand, they can offset and compensate
                  for the direct impact of job destruction through new machinery, lower prices, new
                  investments, and lower wages, among other factors (Vivarelli, 2015). Though pro-
                  cess innovations create job displacement, they create additional labor demand in the
                  upstream industries that produce the new machinery. The net effect of such a mech-
                  anism is vague depending on the efficiency of their relative cost of labor and new
                  machinery. Though process innovations result in employment losses, they also bring
                  about changes that lower average costs. This effect is translated into reduced prices
                  under the assumption of perfect competition, which implies greater demand and
                  hence more production and jobs. However, this line of reasoning breaks down if there
                  are demand rigidities, as untranslated accumulated profit is not converted into new
                  investment (Vivarelli, 1995; Vivarelli, 2007; Vivarelli, 2014; Freeman & Soete, 1987).

                  Empirical evidence
                  There is conflicting empirical evidence on the impact of product, process, and R&D
                  innovation on different segments of employment and their productivity. For instance,
                  Chudnovsky et al. (2006) and Masso and Vahter (2008) found a positive effect of process
                  innovation on productivity. However, Janz et al. (2004) and Van Leeuwen and Klomp
                  (2006a, 2006b) found the opposite. Hall’s (2011) result was a bit different and ambigu-
                  ous relationship between process innovation and productivity, but there was no doubt
                  about the positive effect of product innovation on productivity when he employed the
                  CDM (Crepon, Duguet, and Mairesse) model and measured the real revenue effect.
                  According to him, the ambiguity stems from our inability to quantify process innova-
                  tion’s true quantitative effect—the quantity that matters most for social welfare. Pierre
                  and Hall (2013) have researched the same issues based on the CDM model to confirm
                  a positive relationship between all types of innovation and productivity. But, they held
                  that the impact of innovation varied depending on the production itself and the price at
                  which it is sold. It is challenging to distinguish between these two effects in the lack of
                  reliable individual pricing measurements. Isolating the distinct effects of each form of
                  innovation is challenging due to the imprecise assessment of innovation and its simul-
                  taneity. While pinpointing the precise point of complementarity between them is dif-
                  ficult, there appears to be some complementarity between them. Mairesse and Robin
                  (2009) examine the effect of process, product, and R&D innovations in France through
                  the use of a three-stage econometric model. For both observation periods, we find that
                  worker productivity in the manufacturing industry is greatly and positively enhanced by
                  product innovation. Labor productivity is positively impacted by process innovation as
                  well, although this impact is greater when process innovation is combined with product
                  innovation. Crepon et al. (1998) also concluded the same results about R&D with the
                  demand pull and technology push indicators.
                     Recently, studies have appeared to compare different types of innovation as they are
                  presumed to have different effects on productivity. For instance, Mohnen and Hall
                  (2013) have discussed product innovation and found that it bears a stock of benefit over
                  productivity depending on the degree of innovation to the market and/or to the firm.
                  When it is new to the market that the firm is engaged in, it may have a higher bene-
                  fit in terms of enhancing productivity as it addresses new demand in the market. This
                  may reduce the unit cost and take advantage of economies of scale as a result of product
                  innovation. Following the introduction of a new product to the market, it hurts the old
                  product that existed in the market, so the driving effect of the new one may have a can-
                  nibalizing impact on the old one. As a result, there is a possibility of declining productiv-
                  ity at the initial stage and it may have a positive effect after a certain level in response to
                  learning by using effect. On the other hand, Mairesse et al. (2005) and Hall et al. (2009)
                  found a positive innovation effect on productivity, whereas Raffo et al. (2008) found a
                  negative effect of innovation on productivity. Such inconsistency may arise because of
                  the cannibalizing effect and learning effect.
                     Other than product innovation, some researchers were also interested in examining
                  the effect of process innovation on productivity. Mohnen and Hall (2013) and Masso
                  and Vahter (2008) suggest that process innovation has a positive effect since it is highly
                  correlated with reducing the cost of transactions as expected. However, there is also a
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                       Page 7 of 53




                  possibility of having a negative effect when it has a disruption effect at the beginning due
                  to inefficient production (Roper et al., 2008). On the other hand, Hall (2011) suggested
                  that firms may prefer to perform at their inelastic demand and obtain market power
                  so that they can follow a cost-centered approach and become more cost-efficient. As a
                  result, firms’ sales revenue may be declining (Van Leeuwen and Klomp, 2006a, 2006b). In
                  summary, the empirical findings revealed inconsistent and ambiguous findings regard-
                  ing the effect of process innovation on productivity. Regarding organization and market-
                  ing innovation, it is a prerequisite stage to the success of other innovations and it is a
                  strategic move as an intangible asset. Armbruster et al. (2008) and Greenan (2003) found
                  that it has a positive effect on firm performance as it enhances the quality, competitive-
                  ness, and flexibility of firms’ procedures (Goldman et al., 1995). By using spending on
                  research and development as a traditional proxy, the empirical studies by Mairesse, and
                  Sassenou (1991) and Mairesse and Mohnen (2010) attempted to examine the impact of
                  innovation on firm-level productivity from the input side and discovered that innovation
                  had a positive effect on productivity. Research on the production side of innovation has
                  also been done by Balasubramanian and Sivadasan (2011) and Bloom and Van Reenen
                  (2002), who discovered that patent rights—a proxy for innovation—have a major impact
                  on a firm’s success.
                    Using the World Bank Enterprise Survey in Latin America and the Caribbean, Crespi
                  et al. (2016) found strong evidence regarding the relationships between innovation out-
                  put (product innovation) and productivity as well as between innovation input (R&D)
                  and output (innovation). Additionally, they discovered that process innovation yields
                  smaller private returns than product innovation. They also discovered evidence that
                  spillovers from product innovation are more robust than those from process innovation.
                  A Generalized Structural Equation Model (GSEM) is used to estimate the causal links
                  while accounting for endogeneity. Hanan and Amira (2020) recently found that innova-
                  tion is the key determinant of productivity in all countries, but the evidence is much
                  stronger for product innovation by African firms in comparison with other emerging
                  and developing economies. With a different method of measuring productivity, Naveed
                  and Wang (2023) used a global sample for a panel of 87 countries from 1996 to 2013 with
                  treatments for endogeneity and discovered that innovation had a positive significant
                  effect on both structural change-weighted productivity and unweighted average produc-
                  tivity, with the effect on structural change-weighted productivity being larger. Addition-
                  ally, we discover that this influence varies depending on the income level and structural
                  transition phase of the economy.
                    On the same note, the impact of different types of innovation on employment has
                  been also debatable and generated conflicting results. The influence of innovations on
                  employment has long been a topic of discussion. Researchers have attempted to sepa-
                  rate the impacts of innovation on compensation and displacement, but it is particularly
                  challenging to identify the displacement and compensation effects empirically. The argu-
                  ment provided by Vivarelli (2015) addresses how technological innovation affects the
                  economy through both process and product innovation, both of which can have employ-
                  ment impacts. For the most part, R&D expenditures that result in product innovation
                  are generally labor-friendly, creating new jobs, while embodied technological change
                  that results in process innovation is generally job-destroying. Innovation in products
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                      Page 8 of 53




                  and processes is frequently linked, and job elimination is not always the result of process
                  innovation. Process innovation’s ultimate effect on labor demand is determined by mar-
                  ket mechanisms that, if institutional and market rigidities do not stand in their way, can
                  offset the direct impact of job destruction. Compensation strategies based on price and
                  income have the potential to offset the first worker displacement that follows process
                  innovation.
                    Rethinking previous research that indicated a positive impact of product innova-
                  tion and a lack of a clear pattern for process innovation in developing nations, Doris
                  (2016) discovered that process innovation has an employment-generating effect in
                  Chile, Argentina, and Costa Rica because it may be driven by innovative firms tak-
                  ing market share out of non-innovative firms. According to the Okumu et al. (2019)
                  study, pooled OLS technique data from Africa likewise showed a favorable cor-
                  relation between job growth and process and product innovation. Improvements
                  in processes and products boost worker productivity without directly affecting
                  employment. However, the rise in sales more than offsets this direct and negative
                  impact, meaning that innovation has a net positive impact on employment if the
                  opposing impacts are eliminated. Similarly, Cirera et al., (2016) looked into how
                  product innovation affects labor intensity and generates significant new sales, espe-
                  cially in Africa where businesses are distant from the cutting edge of technology.
                  They also discovered that as a result of production automation, process innova-
                  tion has no short-term detrimental effects on business employment. Additionally,
                  they pointed out that as it increases efficiency and decreases the elasticity of new
                  sales to employment, evidence of a detrimental impact on employment is starting
                  to emerge. It generally has a direct and beneficial impact on employment quantity
                  through innovation, albeit this effect diminishes as businesses move towards the
                  cutting edge of technology.
                    The Chinese experience is a bit different and process innovation exerts a positive
                  effect on employment while product innovation has a negative effect. In particular, the
                  rise in sales of outdated products has a favorable impact on job creation, whereas the
                  rise in productivity of new products has a negative impact. Because diverse labor skill
                  structures affect different enterprises differently, innovation has different effects (Chen
                  et al., 2021). Some of the African experiences (Ghana, Kenya, and Zambia) have shared
                  the conclusion somehow as evidenced by the study (Twumasi et al., 2022). The findings
                  suggest that innovation boosts employment in the subregion. Furthermore, a more sig-
                  nificant source of employment for businesses in sub-Saharan Africa is uniqueness in
                  product invention.



                  Green innovation and sustainability
                  Knowledge has been progressively expanding in recent times at several stages, including
                  limitation, accumulation, concentration, and diffusion. This has resulted in the forma-
                  tion of a distinct kind of invention, innovation, and entrepreneurship, which has allowed
                  businesses to operate in extraordinary ways. The second wave focused on how knowl-
                  edge, social and natural ecosystems, and state strategy could work together to offer the
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                         Page 9 of 53




                  theoretical framework for both practices and policies, current issues with society and
                  development (Giorgos and Manolis, 2021).
                    Innovation is beyond growth perspective since it drives not only economic out-
                  comes, but also addresses sustainable development which is linked to developments in
                  the social, ecological, and economic spheres environmental and social sustainability.
                  Green innovation is the application of innovative ideas to minimize harmful effects on
                  the environment and increase sustainability, tackling global challenges such as climate
                  change and resource depletion. Innovation for sustainable development is a recent phe-
                  nomenon, but its development and implementation are equally complicated, dynamic,
                  and uncertain as other forms of inventions. While it is admirable to want to maximize
                  positive environmental consequences, such innovation must also be financially feasible
                  (Bruno & Diana, 2019).
                    Technological accessibility, institutional frameworks created by governmental regula-
                  tions to manage the transition, and a thorough analysis of the actors involved are all nec-
                  essary for co-optimizing these advancements because innovation does not automatically
                  translate into prosperity and a higher standard of living for all (Vollenbroek, 2001).
                    Given this background, He et al., (2023) and Harrison et al., (2014) assessed the effect of
                  green technology innovation on employment from the viewpoints of environment sustain-
                  ability and industrial structure optimization. The findings show that any form of innovative
                  green technology can dramatically raise employment level. There also exists a positive rela-
                  tionship for firms introducing green innovation voluntarily, as compared to firms comply-
                  ing with regulations (Rasi and Ester, 2016). On the other hand, the green transition’s effects
                  on the labor market are probably going to be uneven across sectors and countries. There
                  may also be significant local economic and distributional implications, which are linked to
                  connected upstream and downstream industries. If labor market frictions prevent work-
                  ers from reallocating their labor, the green transition may have a detrimental impact on
                  overall employment on the other hand (Anneleen et al., 2022). Green innovation is like
                  a two-edged sword when it comes to how it affects firm’s productivity. Technological
                  improvements and sustainability lead to a direct gain in productivity, while inefficiencies,
                  adjustment costs, and disruptions—particularly in the short term—cause an indirect reduc-
                  tion in productivity (Hameed et al., 2024).

                  Method of the study
                  This study employed pseudo-panel data from the World Bank Enterprise Surveys
                  Dataset for selected African countries (Ethiopia, Ghana, Cameroon, Rwanda, Zambia,
                  Senegal, Cote d’Ivoire, Kenya, and Zimbabwe). It also has three methods of analysis to
                  address the impact of innovation on employment and labor productivity.

                  Propensity score matching method
                  As the two-sample t-test failed to figure out the specific impact of treatment on the
                  outcome variables, the propensity score matching method is important in this regard
                  to estimate the specific impact of interventions, exposures, and treatment on outcome
                  variables. It identifies the treatment variables, outcome variables, and covariates (not
                  affected by the program) and computes the probability of treatment on the condition
                  of the observed covariates to balance the covariates between the treated group and the
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                       Page 12 of 53




                  control group, which are the statistical comparison groups. It also allows one to get a
                  specific and unbiased estimate of the average treatment effect (Rosenbaum & Rubin,
                  1983). The computed probability helps to obtain similarities between treated and con-
                  trol groups and matches them based on observed characteristics. The average treatment
                  effect of the intervention can be calculated after matching the treated and non-treated
                  groups given covariates (confounding) that predict receiving the treatment in the speci-
                  fication of the model.
                     In this context, this study takes innovation as a treatment variable and labor produc-
                  tivity and employment as outcome variables, while some characteristics of firms are
                  taken as covariates. The propensity score matching then reduces selection bias that may
                  arise due to a difference in the treatment outcome between the two groups and may be
                  triggered by factors that predict treatment instead of treatment itself. The randomized
                  experiments of the PSM help to reduce treatment assignment bias (unbiased estimation
                  of average treatment effect) as a result of matching the two groups comparable concern-
                  ing the control variables. Thus, the PSM employs a quasi-experimental model and com-
                  putes the average treatment effect (ATE) as it is impossible to compute the individual
                  treatment effect.
                     According to Heckman et al. (1997), ATE = E () = E (–E) (this estimate might not
                  be significant to policymakers for the reason that it includes the effect for whom the
                  involvement was never intended). As a result, the most important valuation parameter
                  is known as the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) = E (T/D = 1)–E (/D = 1).
                  This measures the effect on those for whom the intervention is intended. In a non-exper-
                  imental study, one has to introduce some identifying assumptions to solve the selection
                  problem. Once the two groups are matched based on observation, the average treatment
                  effect of the intervention can then be calculated as the average difference in outcomes
                  between the treated and non-treated units after matching across treated and control
                  groups. This propensity value is estimated based on a statistical model, e.g., a logit or
                  probit model, and thereby estimates the average treatment effect of the outcome differ-
                  ence between the two groups using nearest-neighbor, caliper, stratification, and kernel
                  matching.
                     The other assumption is that there is conditional independence and common support.
                  This overlap condition is defined as P (Ti = 1|Xi) 1. This condition ensures that treatment
                  observations have comparison observations “nearby” in the propensity score distribu-
                  tion. Specifically, the effectiveness of PSM also depends on having a large and roughly
                  equal number of participant and nonparticipant observations so that a substantial
                  region of common support can be found. For estimating the TOT, this assumption can
                  be relaxed to P (Ti = 1|Xi) 1.
                     Treatment units will therefore have to be similar to non-treatment units in terms of
                  observed characteristics unaffected by participation; thus, some non-treatment units
                  may have to be dropped to ensure comparability. However, sometimes a nonrandom
                  subset of the treatment sample may have to be dropped if similar comparison units do
                  not exist. This situation is more problematic because it creates a possible sampling bias in
                  the treatment effect. Heckman et al. (1997) encourage dropping treatment observations
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                       Page 13 of 53




                  with weak common support. Only in the area of common support can inferences be
                  made about causality. This reflects a scenario where common support is weak.
                     Different matching criteria can be used to assign participants to non-participants
                  based on the propensity score. Specifically, nearest-neighbor matching, caliper or radius
                  matching, stratification or interval matching, and local linear matching. One risk with
                  this method is that only a small subset of nonparticipants will finally satisfy the criteria
                  to fall within the common support and thus construct the counterfactual outcome. Non-
                  parametric matching estimators such as kernel matching and LLM use a weighted aver-
                  age of all nonparticipants to construct the counterfactual match for each participant.
                     The quality of the matching procedure is evaluated based on its capability to balance
                  the control and the treatment groups concerning the covariates used for the propen-
                  sity score estimation. There are several procedures for this. The basic idea of all these
                  approaches is to compare the distribution of these covariates in the two groups before
                  and after matching the propensity score. If there are significant differences after match-
                  ing, then matching on the propensity score was not successful in making the groups
                  comparable, and remedial measures have to be taken.
                     A good matching procedure should reduce the standardized bias for each of the covar-
                  iates used in the estimation of the propensity scores. Thus, this approach requires com-
                  paring the standardized bias for each covariate x before and after matching. A similar
                  approach uses a two-sample t-test to check if there are significant differences in covari-
                  ate means for both groups. After matching, the covariates should be balanced in both
                  the treatment and the non-treatment groups. Therefore, no significant difference should
                  be found in the distribution of the covariates between both groups. The pseudo-R2 after
                  matching should be fairly low. The same can be done by checking the F-statistics before
                  and after matching. These statistics indicate the joint significance of all regresses used
                  for the estimation of the propensity score (Caliendo & Kopeining, 2005).
                     It should be clear that matching estimators are not robust against these “hidden
                  biases”. Different scholars have become increasingly aware that it is important to test the
                  robustness of results to depart from the identifying assumption. Since it is not possible
                  to estimate the magnitude of selection bias with non-experimental data, the problem
                  can be addressed by sensitivity analysis. Sensitivity analysis method to address the issue
                  of uncontrolled confounding in observational research. Propensity scores are obtained
                  from observational data that lack randomization. The matching only controls for the
                  differences in the observed variables, and there may be some bias resulting from the
                  unobserved covariates that could affect whether subjects receive treatment or not. Esti-
                  mation-based PSM is unbiased if there are no unmeasured confounders and if all rele-
                  vant covariates have been incorporated into the estimation model. If there are unknown
                  variables that affect both treatment assignment and the outcome variable, a hidden bias
                  may occur for which matching estimators are not robust. The sensitivity parameter
                  (gamma) also measures the degree of departure from random assignment of treatment.
                  It is a measure of departure from the study that is free of bias. In a randomized experi-
                  ment, randomization of the treatment ensures that it is equal to 1, no sensitivity analy-
                  ses are required. However, the value of gamma is unknown for observational studies;
                  sensitivity analysis requires a trial of several values of gamma to see how the conclusion
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                          Page 14 of 53




                  might change. The concern in sensitivity analysis is whether or not an inference about
                  treatment effect may be altered by unobserved factors.



                  Dose–response model
                  This model was proposed by Hirano and Imbens (2004) to evaluate the impact of pro-
                  grams or interventions with continuous treatment exposure. It is also an experimental
                  condition and estimates a dose–response function using the generalized propensity
                  score (GPS). It is an extension of the popular propensity score methodology for binary
                  treatment group assignments (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983) and multivalued treatments
                  (Imbens, 2000; Lechner, 2001). Indeed, Hirano and Imbens (2004) show that the GPS
                  has a balancing property similar to the binary propensity score. Conditional on observ-
                  able characteristics, the level of treatment can be considered random for units belonging
                  to the same GPS strata. This means that adjusting for the GPS removes all biases associ-
                  ated with differences in the covariates.
                     A dose–response function (DRF) can be calculated using a regression approach when
                  i) treatment is continuous; ii) individuals react differently to observable confounders;
                  and iii) treatment selection is endogenous. From a program evaluation perspective,
                  indeed, what is relevant in many settings is not only the binary treatment status, but also
                  the level of exposure (or dose) provided by a public agency. The DRFs are usually esti-
                  mated to check respondents’ resilience to different levels of intervention.
                     Consider a policy program where the treatment is not assigned randomly (that is, it
                  is assigned according to some structural rule) and where—after setting who is treated
                  and who is not—the program provides a different level of exposure to treatment, rang-
                  ing from 0 (no treatment) to 100 (maximum level of treatment). Two groups of units are
                  thus formed: untreated, whose level of treatment (or dose) is 0, and treated, whose level
                  of treatment is greater than 0. We are interested in estimating the causal effect of the
                  treatment variable t on an outcome y within the observed sample, assuming that treated
                  and untreated units may respond differently both to specific observable confounders
                  (that we collect in a vector x) and to the intensity of the treatment t. We wish to estimate
                  a DRF of y on t when the treatment is assumed to be either exogenous (that is, selection
                  into treatment depends only on factors observable to the analyst) or endogenous (that
                  is, selection into treatment depends on factors both observable and unobservable to the
                  analyst).
                     Compared with similar models—and in particular, the one proposed by Hirano and
                  Imbens (2004) and implemented in Stata by Bia and Mattei (2008)—this model does not
                  need a full normality assumption, and it is well suited when many individuals have a
                  treatment level of 0. Moreover, it may account for treatment endogeneity by exploiting
                  instrumental variables (IV) estimation in a continuous treatment context. When many
                  units are not exposed to treatment, the distribution of t has a “spike” or non-nil prob-
                  ability mass at 0, that is, p(t = 0) > 0. As a result, assuming that the distribution of t|x is
                  normal (or a mixture of normal distributions), as assumed in Hirano and Imbens’s (2004)
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship       (2025) 14:9                                                     Page 15 of 53




                  generalized propensity score (GPS), is untenable, because in the presence of a spike at
                  0, this distribution is discontinuous and thus nonnormal. Guardabascio and Ventura
                  (2014), on the other hand, recently proposed a modification to the Hirano–Imbens
                  model that extends the GPS approach to the case of a nonnormal continuous treat-
                  ment variable. The authors consider a set of alternative distributions (binomial, Pois-
                  son, gamma, inverse Gaussian, etc.) derived from the exponential family of distributions.
                  Similarly, Bia et al. (2014) have proposed a generalization of the Hirano–Imbens model
                  that allows one to estimate semiparametrically the DRF. In their article, the authors
                  propose to estimate the GPS parametrically under various alternative distributional
                  assumptions (such as the normal, inverse Gaussian, and gamma distributions) using as
                  link functions the identity function, the logarithm, and the power function. Moreover, in
                  the case of a treatment variable assuming values in the (0, 1) interval, a two-parameter
                  Beta distribution is also implemented.
                     Another interesting approach to modeling a continuous treatment setting is the one
                  proposed by Adorno et al. (2007). They propose a nonparametric two-step match-
                  ing approach based on the generalized propensity function developed by Imai and van
                  Dyk (2004). In the first step, they specify a participation decision rule and match units
                  on the set of covariates that identify such a process; in the second step, by consider-
                  ing only matched units from the first step, they perform another matching procedure,
                  pairing units having similar values only for the covariates explaining the treatment
                  level assignment. Treated and untreated units are thus assumed to be balanced in both
                  processes (binary selection and level assignment) because the only difference between
                  the two groups should be in program participation. Although this model considers the
                  presence of “zeros” in the treatment level variable, it is suitable only under conditional
                  independence.
                     However, the approach presented in this article has some of its limitations. First, dif-
                  ferently from the semiparametric approach adopted by Adorno et al. (2007) and Bia
                  et al. (2014), the approach presented here assumes a parametric form of the potential
                  outcomes with additive separability. Second, it does not need an estimation of the GPS,
                  which might be interesting to analyze itself. Third, the proposed IV estimation uses a
                  Heckman bivariate selection model, which requires additional distributional assump-
                  tions. Fourth, it only considers the presence of observable heterogeneity, thus neglecting
                  the possible presence of unobservable heterogeneity. The model is, however, well suited
                  to address DRF estimation when conditions (i) and (ii) above are present.

                  Impact of the overall combined innovation (refer Appendix B)
                  This section presents, using propensity score matching (PSM), the overall combined
                  effects of all types of innovations (product, process, and R&D) on the employment and
                  productivity of various workforce segments. The findings indicate that while all forms
                  of innovations—process, product, and R&D—have a favorable overall impact on the

                  lower labor productivity in its early phases because of the new product’s deriving effect
                  on the old one and the disruption effect at the beginning caused by inefficient produc-
                  tion. Furthermore, this result is consistent with the descriptive analysis, which indicates
                  that labor productivity may be significantly harmed by innovation. However, as Appen-
                  dix B illustrates, innovation may have a major positive effect on production workers’
                  productivity. While innovation contributed to increased employment, particularly in the
                  production sector, it led to a decrease in labor productivity, especially among skilled and
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                         Page 20 of 53




                  non-production workers. This suggests that the benefits of innovation in terms of job
                  creation are offset by initial inefficiencies or disruption in work processes.
                    This result supports the claim made by Mairesse, and Sassenou (1991), Hall et al.
                  (2009), Mairesse and Mohnen (2010), and Mairesse et al. (2005) that innovation, espe-
                  cially when it involves a new product or service, can significantly increase labor pro-
                  ductivity by meeting market demand, lowering unit costs, and utilizing economies of
                  scale. Several academics, such as Avenyo et al. (2019), contend that innovation may have
                  a larger effect on job growth than labor productivity alone. The PSM results showed that
                  innovation has a statistically significant overall and combined effect that benefits skilled
                  and non-production workers, suggesting that innovation increases demand for these
                  workers. This result is also in line with the research by Guillermo et al. (2020), which
                  found that high-skilled workers benefit significantly more from innovation in the work-
                  place than do low-skilled people. The findings imply that innovation, particularly in the
                  area of products, promotes a bias in favor of high-skilled job demands at the expense of
                  low-skilled individuals. It is also consistent with the claims made by Avenyo et al. (2019)
                  that innovation has the potential to greatly boost employment, especially in medium-
                  and low-tech industries where sub-Saharan Africa is well-known for its firm structure.
                  According to Okumu et al. (2019) and Cirera and Sabetti (2016), additional sales linked
                  to innovation are probably going to be produced at the same or higher labor intensity.
                  Furthermore, in sub-Saharan Africa, where businesses are less connected to cutting-
                  edge technology, this noteworthy beneficial impact is far stronger (Cirera & Sabetti,
                  2016).

                  Impact of the product innovation (refer Appendix C)
                  The productivity of all labor segments—permanent, production, non-production,
                  skilled, and unskilled workers—is statistically significantly impacted by product innova-
                  tion, but only the productivity of production workers is positively impacted. This raises
                  some concerns about why it negatively affects other segments of labor. Product innova-
                  tion may potentially have a short-term negative impact on productivity since changes
                  take time to implement. Furthermore, it might take a while for new items to get mar-
                  ket recognition and there is no guarantee that they will be successful on the market.
                  Additionally, it has been proposed that the detrimental effects of tailored production on
                  productivity are likely to be triggered by new products (Roper et al., 2008; Tomasz &
                  Arkadiusz, 2018).
                     Furthermore, except for skilled workers, product innovation has no statistical signifi-
                  cance and little ability to affect any segments of the labor market. This can be reasoned
                  out to the fact that the net effect of direct effect and indirect can be fully settled by each
                  other in quantitative terms. This means that new items that are manufactured more effi-
                  ciently than old products may require less labor input for a given output. This can nul-
                  lify the impact of product innovation that has a positive direct labor effect by creating
                  new demand (Pianta, 2005; Vivarelli, 1995). Furthermore, the impact of new products
                  on labor demand varies depending on whether they are complementary or substitutes.
                  For example, a firm needs less labor if the demand for new items can partially replace
                  the demand for old products. If they are complementary, the firm needs more work
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship   (2025) 14:9                                     Page 21 of 53




                  otherwise. Therefore, if firms in industry engage in both sorts of products, the effect
                  could be null on employment on average (Peters et al., 2017).


                  Impact of the process innovation (refer Appendix D)
                  It shows that, on average, process innovation has a statistically significant positive
                  impact on the productivity of production workers, while hurting the productivity of
                  skilled, non-production, and permanent workers. After matching, the productivity of
                  the skilled, unskilled, and nonproduction-treated groups is lower than that of the non-
                  treated groups. The sole distinction is that the impact’s magnitudes vary depending on
                  the kind of labor group.
                     This PSM conclusion further reinforces the claim made by Hall (2011) and Roper
                  et al. (2008) that early innovation may lower labor productivity because of the way the
                  new depends on the old and because of the disruption caused by inefficient manufac-
                  turing. Furthermore, this result is consistent with the descriptive analysis (see Table 3b
                  in Appendix A), which indicates that labor productivity may be significantly harmed by
                  innovation.
                     Conversely, innovation could significantly increase the productivity of workers in the
                  production area. After matching, it is discovered that the treated groups are more pro-
                  ductive than the non-treated groups. This result supports the claims made by Mohnen
                  and Hall (2013) and Masso and Vahter (2008) that process innovation, which is posi-
                  tively connected with lower transaction costs, may boost worker productivity. Further-
                  more, research from Hall, Mairesse, and Sassenou (1991), Hall et al. (2009), Mairesse
                  and Mohnen (2010), and Mairesse et al. (2005) suggest that innovation, especially when
                  it addresses a new market demand, lowers unit costs, and takes advantage of economies
                  of scale, may have a significant positive impact on labor productivity. All of the afore-
                  mentioned data collectively demonstrate the fallacy of claims made regarding the rela-
                  tionship between labor productivity and innovation.
                     Process innovation has a positive effect on the employment of skilled, non-skilled,
                  and production workers. This result is consistent with the claims made by Avenyo et al.
                  (2019) that innovation has the potential to greatly boost employment, especially in
                  medium- and low-tech industries where sub-Saharan Africa is well-known for its firm
                  structure. The claims made by Okumu et al. (2019) and Cirera and Sabetti (2016) all sup-
                  port the idea that process innovation has the potential to increase employment because
                  the new sales it generates will probably have a labor intensity that is equal to or higher
                  than before.


                  Impact of R&D innovation (refer Appendix E)
                  Permanent, non-production, skilled, and unskilled worker productivity does not posi-
                  tively react to R&D innovation. Given that internal and external circumstances may
                  cause its impact to occur early on, this negative influence may materialize in the short
                  run. Given that long-term economic growth is thought to originate from R&D innova-
                  tion, it might end up being advantageous in the long run. This result goes against the
                  claims made by Mairesse, and Sassenou (1991) and Mairesse and Mohnen (2010) and is
                  in support with the claims made by Hall (2011) and Roper et al. (2008).

                  Results of the dose–response estimation (refer Appendix F)
                  This study uses a dose–response estimation technique in addition to PSM to examine
                  the possible effects of treatment intensity on labor productivity and employment in the
                  chosen sub-Saharan African nations. According to the plot of the dose–response func-
                  tion (DRF), the relationship between intensity and productivity of permanent employ-
                  ees is strongly declining and fairly accurately estimated for lower values of the dose, for
                  higher levels of the dose, the relationship is more strongly declining but less accurately
                  estimated. Considering the remaining DRFs, the productivity of production, non-pro-
                  duction, skilled, and unskilled workers decreases as innovation intensity increases. All
                  of these findings are in line with the propensity score matching method (PSM), except
                  for how combined and total innovation affects production workers. In other words,
                  the intensity has a declining DRF effect while innovation has growing (positive) effects
                  by PSM for production workers. Some of the findings are in favor of Mohnen and Hall
                  (2013), Duguet (2006), and Roper et al. (2008) arguments that, particularly at the initial
                  stages of innovation, labor productivity may diminish in innovative firms. In addition,
                  most of the findings are consistent with the above arguments under PSM and descriptive
                  statistics, which attests to the robustness of the estimation results.
                    Aside from innovation (the treatment variable), firm age, having an international
                  quality certificate, total main product, foreign private ownership, and number of
                  working hours per week all have a significant positive impact on labor productivity.
                  Nonetheless, power shortages, government ownership, and domestic private owner-
                  ship decrease the productivity of employees significantly. This can be because for-
                  eign individuals, companies, and organizations may have more access to modern
                  methods of products, technologies, and organizational developments, which have a

                  potentially positive effect on labor productivity compared to domestic private compa-
                  nies, organizations, and governments. This argument is consistent with the argument
                  from descriptive statistics that foreign private ownership has a positive correlation
                  with labor productivity. This finding is also compatible with the arguments from Grif-
                  fith et al. (2004), and Mebratie and Bedi (2013) that firms’ age, foreign ownership,
                  and internationally recognized quality certificates have a significant positive impact
                  on labor productivity.
                     Regarding intensity impact on employment, the DRF also indicates that there is a
                  strong relationship between the intensity of innovation and permanent employees as

                  production workers. But the general trend is increasing. For both non-production work-

                  However, the intensity of innovation drives increasing effects for skilled workers. In gen-

                  positive employment effects, especially in firms engaging heavily in innovation. How-
                  ever, increased innovation also correlates with a greater negative impact on labor pro-

                     Some of these findings are in line with the arguments from Avenyo et al., (2019),

                  with the findings from propensity score matching (PSM). However, the employment
                  of unskilled production workers decreases as the dose of innovation increases. How-

                  around five doses of innovation, their relationship becomes positive. This also shows
                  the inconsistency of arguments on employment and innovation.

                  Conclusion and the way forward
                  African countries have been attempting to face the deeply rooted problems of perpetual
                  economic growth and economic transformation through a range of reforms and rede-
                  signed industrialization policies to enhance firms’ productivity, widen employment
                  opportunities, and establish a broad-based economy. To do so, innovation is considered
                  one of the integral parts and the means to sustain the growth of manufacturing sectors.
                  It consists of various innovations, including product innovation, process innovation, and
                  RD innovation, by converting ideas and knowledge into production processes through
                  learning by doing, experiences, exposures, attitudes, skills, and economies of scale,
                  which lead to enhanced market position and economic growth in the framework of a
                  knowledge-based economy.
                     The study employs World Bank Enterprise Survey data for Ethiopia, Ghana, Cam-
                  eroon, Rwanda, Zambia, Senegal, Cote d’Ivoire, Kenya, and Zimbabwe and creates
                  pseudo-panel data with the specific objective of examining the impact of innovation
                  on employment and labor productivity using propensity score matching and the dose–
                  response method. More than 90% of sample firms report at least one innovation, includ-
                  ing a new product or service, a new marketing strategy, a new manufacturing process, or
                  a new organizational structure. This means that 2,673 firms are engaged in at least one of
                  the innovation activities and 250 firms do not have any innovation activities.
                     The study finds that process innovation has a statistically significant impact on produc-
                  tion, non-production, and skilled employees. And, it has a positive effect on the produc-
                  tivity of production workers and a negative effect on the productivity of non-production
                  and skilled employees. However, it has a positive and statistically significant impact on
                  these three labor types in terms of employment. R&D innovation, on the other hand,
                  has a 10% negative and statistically significant impact on the productivity of permanent,
                  non-production, skilled, and unskilled workers. And, R&D innovation has a positive and
                  statistically significant impact on creating employment opportunities for production,
                  non-production, skilled and unskilled employees.
                     Finally, this research ends and adds a great deal to our knowledge of how innovation
                  affects employment and labor productivity in African economies. Its casts doubt on the
                  notion that innovation always boosts output by exposing a more nuanced dynamic in
                  which the creation of jobs is accompanied by decreased productivity in some labor mar-
                  kets. The results highlight how crucial it is to back up innovative policies that solve pro-
                  ductivity issues and increase employment. For academics, decision-makers, and business
                  executives who want to support innovation-driven growth in developing nations, this
                  report is an invaluable resource.

                  Policy implications
                  Approximately 90% of the African firms that were chosen for the World Bank Enterprise
                  Survey have been trying to continue engaging in innovative activities. They are, however,
                  falling behind others in terms of depth of innovation. There is also remarkable progress
                  in certain activities though it results in a mixed effect on productivity and job creation.
                    On its own, innovation is beneficial and has the potential to have a significant posi-
                  tive economic impact. However, in contrast to its potential, this study finds contradic-
                  tory results regarding the influence of innovation on employment and productivity in
                  the African environment. One of the policy implications is that there must be a strategy
                  of capitalizing on the positive impact of innovation on the productivity and employment
                  of different labor segments, and think about the way how to reverse the negative impact
                  of innovation on productivity and employment by providing due attention to the driv-
                  ing factors and explanations behind the results. It means that it should discover the fac-
                  tors and explanations that might diverge or converge with their possible influence on
                  employment and productivity such as lengthy adjustment time, market imperfection,
                  returns of innovation, and complementarity and substitutability nature of innovation
                  products. Consequently, the results, as well as these explanations, must be considered
                  when designing innovation policies, labor market policies, and economic develop-
                  ment initiatives in the chosen nations. Routines are a firm’s genetic code, yet innovation
                  policies and labor strategies are not as detail-oriented in Africa. Therefore, rather than
                  implementing uniform and aggregated policies with a linear approach, the policy should
                  capture the complex character of innovation and its accompanying impact on productiv-
                  ity and labor forces So, the study would inform the policy settlements to be disaggre-
                  gated into distinct segments of labor outcomes.
                    Rethinking how innovation is adjusted and how it deviates from its intended purpose
                  in increasing productivity and job creation should also be part of the policy prescription.
                  This problem deals with shortening the time it takes for a product to go from idea to
                  market availability, which promotes a positive indirect effect of innovation. The adjust-
                  ment period is too protracted in the African context, resulting in a long lag structure.
                  This would result in a high transaction cost, defects, wastes, and detrimental effects
                  from both internal and external business-facilitating factors in the activities of product
                  and R&D innovation. To counteract the detrimental effects of specific product and R&D
                  innovations, policies and strategies should take this into account and shorten the adjust-
                  ment time and lag structure by expanding provisions like soft and hard infrastructures.
                  Otherwise, it leaves the market success of new products with uncertainty.
                    Besides, there must be a policy framework to manage both the direct and indirect
                  effects of innovation to reverse its negative influence on productivity and employment.
                  In actuality, the majority of African policies prioritize the direct impact above the indi-
                  rect one. Product innovation is supposed to have a positive direct labor effect, whereas
                  process innovation is thought to have a negative influence on employment. However, the
                  indirect effects (i.e., the response or feedback of the market to innovation itself ) might
                  mitigate the favorable and unfavorable labor outcomes. For instance, the increased labor
                  demand for product innovation might be challenged by the indirect effect if the new
                  products are more efficient, and reduce the labor demand. In the same fashion, the nega-
                  tive direct labor effect of process innovations can be reversed by the indirect effect of
Sime and Tadesse ﻿Journal of Innovation and Entrepreneurship         (2025) 14:9                                         Page 26 of 53




                  increasing production efficiency. In short, the response of the market and its capabil-
                  ity of transmission to influence productivity and employment must be considered in the
                  policy framework. The key question is: in what market conditions is innovation being
                  implemented? Since wage and price rigidity is the primary element influencing innova-
                  tion’s function, the policy framework should take this into account depending on the
                  specific nature country’s context. Furthermore, the type of a good—whether it is substi-
                  tutable or complimentary to an existing product—has implications for how innovation
                  affects employment. While the complimentary character increases the demand for labor,
                  the substitutability nature reduces the requirement for more labor. So, the policy frame-
                  work should reconsider this issue and design a compensation mechanism accordingly to
                  keep productivity and employment vibrant.
                    The nature of the information obtained from innovation surveys is the subject of part
                  of policy implication. There are many reasons to reconsider it. Among them are, but are
                  not limited to, the dummy variable’s intrinsic imprecision for innovation, the absence of
                  pertinent variables, and productivity measurements have limited doing a thorough study
                  that accurately reflects reality. Therefore, to obtain relevant data and wise policy recom-

                  Limitations and areas of future research
                  One of the study’s shortcomings is that the relevant data for the African nations that
                  were left out were unavailable. The World Bank Survey’s content also prevents the com-
                  putation of alternative measures for a firm’s productivity and innovation. In light of
                  this restriction, future studies could concentrate on creating analytical techniques that
                  integrate the general structural equation model (GSEM) and propensity score matching
                  (PSM) to examine the mediating effects of adjustment time, market imperfection, and
                  the complementarity and substitutability of products in the nexus of innovation, pro-
                  ductivity, and employment. And, the future will be more demanding for having a com-
                  parative analysis of PSM and GSEM for those firms with and without green innovation.
