# 1. Introduction

Whether a management practice works the same way wherever it is adopted is a core question of general management research. Universalist accounts treat management as a technology whose adoption raises productivity (Bloom & Van Reenen, 2007; Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, 2016). Contingency theory holds that there is no single best way and that performance depends on fit with conditions (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Donaldson, 2001). Institutional theory adds that practices may be adopted for legitimacy and remain decoupled from what happens in operations (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Zbaracki, 1998), and work on the transfer of practices shows that success depends on institutionalisation in the receiving unit and on its institutional and organisational context (Kostova, 1999).

This article first interprets evidence on observable management practices through these general frameworks and then relates the interpretation to the management control literature. We use the management-practices module of the World Bank Enterprise Surveys (WBES) for seven G7 economies in 2024–2025 (6,316 establishments) and ask: to what extent are the structure and the performance associations of observable management practices universal across advanced economies, and to what extent are they contingent on national and organisational contexts?

Four ideas are kept apart: universality of *practices* (observed everywhere), of *structure* (a common statistical dimension), of *effectiveness* (a stable association with performance) and *contextual fit* (effectiveness depends on alignment with context). Only the first three can be examined with WBES; contextual fit is treated as a theoretical interpretation. The paper preserves the findings of an earlier exploratory phase, in which practices were organised predominantly along a continuous dimension rather than in distinct latent classes, and adds analyses of measurement comparability, cross-country heterogeneity, firm-level contingencies, equifinality and level dependence. The management control literature (Malmi & Brown, 2008; Grabner & Moers, 2013; Simons, 1995) enters in a second step, as a domain-specific counterpart of the general concepts of adoption, integration and use.
