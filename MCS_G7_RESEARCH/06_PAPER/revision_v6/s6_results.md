# 6. Results

**6.1 Sample (Table 1).** The module universe contains 6,316 establishments. Reported use of monitoring ranges from 47% in Japan to 92% in the United Kingdom, targets from 60% in Italy to 93% in the United Kingdom, and target-linked bonuses from 36% in Italy to 76% in the United Kingdom. These descriptive differences are confounded by size composition and are not evidence of institutional or cultural effects.

**6.2 Individual associations (Table 2).** With the three practices entered together, incentives show the strongest association with productivity (0.199 log points, 95% CI 0.156 to 0.243, about 22% higher sales per employee), followed by monitoring (0.122; 0.069 to 0.175; about 13%); targets are not distinguishable from zero (0.047; −0.010 to 0.104), so the expectation that each practice is positively associated with productivity (H1) is not supported for targets. For sales growth, targets (0.0077) and incentives (0.0064) are positively associated and monitoring is not; for employment growth only incentives are (0.0079; 0.0044 to 0.0113). Using the breadth of monitoring (three bands of r3) instead of its presence, each band is associated with 0.085 log points of productivity (0.063 to 0.107), and the association of targets falls to 0.019. These are conditional associations within sector-by-country cells; they do not show that adopting a practice raises performance.

**6.3 Combinations (Table 3).** No pairwise interaction is distinguishable from zero for productivity or sales growth, and the joint pairwise test is not significant for any outcome (p = 0.35, 0.94, 0.14). The three-way interaction is positive for productivity (0.229; 0.010 to 0.448; p = 0.040) but is absent for the growth outcomes (p = 0.99, 0.84) and does not survive correction for the three outcomes tested. Given the partial overlap of the targets and incentives items (Section 4), the data provide no evidence that the practices operate jointly beyond their individual associations.

**6.4 Stability across countries (Tables 4 to 6, Figure 2).** For productivity, the estimated association of incentives varies from 0.509 in Canada (0.333 to 0.684; about 66%) to 0.048 in France (−0.079 to 0.175; about 5%), with 0.360 in Italy, 0.225 in Germany, 0.202 in the United Kingdom, 0.154 in the United States and 0.082 in Japan (−0.006 to 0.171); Cochran's Q = 28.4 (df = 6, p = 0.0001), I² = 79% (95% CI 58% to 97%), τ = 0.114 (95% CI 0.070 to 0.346). The association of monitoring is positive in the United States (0.193), Japan (0.146) and Germany (0.089) and not distinguishable from zero in the other four countries, and that of targets in none; equality across countries is not rejected for either (p = 0.39 and 0.63 with HC3 errors). Descriptively, the productivity association is concentrated in incentives in Canada and Italy and is of similar size for monitoring and incentives in the United States and Japan, but differences between practice coefficients within a country were not tested.
The evidence for heterogeneity in the incentives–productivity association is moderate and depends on inference (Table 6). It is unchanged by leaving out any single country (p from 0.0000 to 0.013; I² from 65% to 82%; without Canada Q = 14.5, I² = 65%), by not truncating the outcome, by 5/95 truncation and by median regression (I² 77% to 81%), so outliers do not drive it. It is weaker with the supplied weights (Q = 11.0, p = 0.088, I² = 45%) and with the restricted wild cluster bootstrap, where the p-value for incentives and productivity is 0.015 (73 clusters, 7 to 15 per country) and 0.105 after Holm correction over the nine tests; the asymptotic clustered p-values of 0.0000 in earlier versions were anti-conservative and are not reported. For growth, the wild bootstrap p-values are 0.006 for targets and sales growth (Holm 0.054) and 0.012 for targets and employment growth (Holm 0.096); no test is significant at the 5% level after Holm correction under the bootstrap. Heterogeneity in incentives is therefore large in magnitude, robust to outliers and to the exclusion of any country, but statistically fragile under weighting and clustered inference. The expectation that associations differ across countries (H3) is suggestively supported for incentives and productivity only.

**6.5 Organisational contingencies (Table 7).** Among the six organisational moderators, only size shows a joint interaction with the practices for productivity (Wald χ² = 11.5, p = 0.009), which does not survive correction over the 18 tests (Holm p = 0.17); foreign ownership (p = 0.048; Holm 0.71) and the other moderators show no reliable moderation, and none is significant for growth after correction.

**6.6 Out-of-sample comparison (Table 8).** For productivity, common slopes for the three practices (P1) lower out-of-sample RMSE from 0.7924 to 0.7854, a 1.8% reduction in mean squared error (corrected 95% CI 0.0055 to 0.0167 in squared log points; p < 0.001). Adding combinations (−0.0002), organisational contingency (−0.0010) or country-specific slopes for each practice (N1; 0.0002; corrected CI −0.0031 to 0.0035; p = 0.91) brings no detectable gain. The partial-pooling model (HB) has the lowest RMSE (0.7845), 0.0014 below P1 (corrected CI −0.0003 to 0.0031; p = 0.11) and 0.0012 below N1 (−0.0009 to 0.0033; p = 0.25): the best point estimate, but not reliably different from either. For growth outcomes no model improves on the controls by more than 0.25%. These results show that the comparison could not detect gains, not that gains are absent: the corrected interval for N1 against P1 includes both zero and the oracle gain of 0.0028 described below, and equivalence is not established.

**6.7 Why does a common model remain competitive?** Heterogeneity that is large in magnitude is small in predictive terms because the practices explain little variance. If country-specific slopes were known exactly, the mean squared error would fall by 0.0028 (0.45% of the residual variance), almost entirely through incentives, whereas estimating eighteen additional slopes costs about 0.0021 out of sample; the expected net gain is 0.0008. In simulations at the estimated heterogeneity, national slopes beat the common model in only 53% of replications (mean gain 0.0007); at twice the estimated heterogeneity they do in 95%. With seven countries and R² of about 2%, an out-of-sample comparison therefore has roughly even power at the estimated heterogeneity, and shrinkage (HB), which sits between the two extremes, gives the best point estimate. The competitiveness of the common model reflects the small share of variance explained and the cost of estimating country-specific slopes; it does not show that associations are homogeneous.

**6.8 Transferability (Table 9).** Out-of-sample R² for productivity is small in every country (local models: −0.02 to 0.08). A model estimated in a single country is worse than the local model in 93% of the 42 source–target pairs, by 0.033 R² units on average, with large losses for some pairs (Canada and Italy as sources for France: −0.118 and −0.111). Holding the control coefficients fixed at the target's LOCO estimates so that only the practice coefficients are transferred, the local model is better in 81% of pairs with a mean advantage of 0.017, and the loss for Canada to France remains (−0.113); about half of the loss in the full model therefore comes from the control coefficients and composition, and the rest from the practice coefficients. With a single latent score of practices, the local model was better in 88% of pairs (mean advantage 0.020; supplementary analysis), so single-country transfer of the practice-level model loses more than that of the global score, but the two analyses use different predictors. A model estimated on the other six countries (LOCO) performs close to the local model but not identically: local minus LOCO is positive in six of seven countries (0.001 to 0.022), each interval includes zero, and the inverse-variance combined difference is 0.0095 R² units (95% CI 0.0023 to 0.0167; p = 0.010), a small but detectable advantage of local estimation. For practice-only transfer the local-minus-LOCO differences are close to zero (−0.005 to 0.015; every interval includes zero). Growth outcomes show no transferable signal.

**6.9 Complementary analyses of latent structure (retained from the earlier analysis).** For the six items asked of all firms (r1, r2, r4, r8, r10, r11), the items follow one dominant continuous dimension (parallel analysis: eigenvalue 2.54 versus 1.09), latent classes are poorly separated (relative entropy 0.53 to 0.60), and continuous or hybrid models fit better than purely discrete models (BIC differences of 129 to 368 points); no framework-implied split of seven items with verified or partly verified wording (r1, r2, r3, r4, r5, r8, r9) improves on a single factor (BIC +8.5 to +43.4). Cross-country invariance is partial and depends on the number of classes, and the reliability of the latent score varies across countries from 0.35 to 0.65 depending on the prior used. With the latent score, productivity slopes are positive in most countries, heterogeneous in most specifications and flatten at high levels of the score (0.164 in the lower third, 0.013 in the upper third); allowing a common level-dependent shape reduces cross-country heterogeneity, so part of it may reflect adoption level, ceiling or measurement error. A sensitivity analysis that excludes r10 and r11 (the seven items just listed) gives the same conclusions: a common latent slope lowers out-of-sample mean squared error by 1.4% (corrected 95% CI 0.0044 to 0.0137), contingency, country-specific slopes and level-dependent shapes bring no reliable gain (corrected p of at least 0.39), and the reliability of this score by country is 0.47 to 0.67 with a pooled prior. These analyses support the use of the three practice indicators as separate variables: a single score masks the practice-specific heterogeneity found in Section 6.4.
