                  preserving natural resources, minimizing environmental degradation, and enhancing
                  social welfare and long-term productivity. At the firm level, implementing GEPs such as
                  energy-efficient technologies, water recycling, and waste minimization enables firms to
                  enhance resource productivity [1], strengthen competitiveness [2], and improve overall
                  performance [3]. Moreover, the integration of GEPs aligns with national commitments
                  to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 8 (Decent Work and
                  Economic Growth) and SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production). Firms that
                  embrace GEPs also gain greater legitimacy among stakeholders [4] by demonstrating
                  environmental responsibility and compliance with Environmental, Social, and Gover-
                  nance (ESG) standards. Ultimately, the adoption of GEPs should be viewed not only as
                  an environmental mandate but as a strategic necessity for achieving long-term sustain-
                  ability and competitiveness.
                     Innovation plays a pivotal role in enhancing firm competitiveness [5] and driving
                  long-term growth [6]. A substantial body of literature suggests that firm innovation is
                  shaped by a wide range of internal and external factors, including financial resources [7],
                  corporate governance mechanisms [8], market competition [9], and institutional qual-
                  ity and regulatory frameworks [10]. In the current global economic environment, firms
                  must continuously innovate to enhance efficiency, develop new products and processes,
                  and sustain competitive advantage. Such efforts enable firms to respond proactively to
                  market changes, regulatory pressures, and evolving consumer expectations, while trans-
                  forming environmental and social challenges into opportunities for value creation [11].
                     Against this backdrop, the adoption of GEPs has emerged as an important driver of
                  innovation. By integrating environmental practices into their operations and strategies,
                  firms can address regulatory and societal demands [12] and build dynamic capabilities
                  that support product differentiation and process improvement. Accordingly, the adop-
                  tion of GEPs not only contributes to environmental objectives but may also enhance
                  firms’ innovative capacity, reinforce competitiveness, and promote sustainable economic
                  performance. Building on this perspective, this study investigates the impact of GEP
                  adoption on firm innovation using a World Bank Enterprise Surveys (WBES) database.
                  A key advantage of the WBES database is that it enables researchers to measure firms’
                  engagement in green economy practices in a detailed and standardized manner. Spe-
                  cifically, the WBES includes a Green Economy module, which captures multiple aspects
                  of firms’ environmental management. This module identifies ten dimensions of GEPs:
                  (1) heating and cooling improvements; (2) climate-friendly on-site energy generation;
                  (3) machinery upgrades; (4) energy management systems; (5) waste minimization, recy-
                  cling, and waste management; (6) air pollution control measures; (7) water management
                  measures; (8) upgrades of vehicles, vessels, or aircraft within the firm’s fleet; (9) improve-
                  ments in lighting systems; and (10) other pollution control measures. This detailed clas-
                  sification enables a more granular investigation of whether and which specific green
                  economy practices are associated with firm-level innovation.
                     By empirically examining the relationship between GEPs and firm innovation, this
                  study makes several important contributions to the literature. First, this study extends
                  existing research by focusing on firms’ adoption of specific GEPs and their direct effects
                  on firm-level innovation. This aspect has received limited attention. In contrast, much
                  of the prior literature has emphasized sustainability or environmental performance

                  outcomes [13] rather than innovation-related consequences. Furthermore, previous
                  studies have often relied on proxy measures of environmental engagement, such as cor-
                  porate social responsibility (CSR) ratings [14] or ISO 14000 certification [15], rather than
                  on the actual implementation of green practices. Second, by examining firms’ adoption
                  across ten distinct dimensions of GEPs, this study enables a more granular assessment

                  and firm innovation, thereby addressing a key gap in the literature, which has largely
                  relied on single-country analyses [16, 17]. Drawing on a diverse sample of firms oper-

                  generalizable insights into how GEPs influence innovation outcomes. This broader per-

                  stimulating firm-level innovation.

                  the empirical results and provides a detailed discussion. Section 5 offers the conclusion.

                  2 Literature review
                  Many studies have examined the determinants of firm innovation, highlighting the roles
                  of both internal firm characteristics and external environmental factors. Prior studies
                  emphasize internal drivers such as financial resources [7], ownership structure [18], or
                  corporate governance [8], which influence a firm’s ability and willingness to invest in
                  innovative activities. Beyond firm-specific factors, external conditions, including mar-
                  ket competition [9] and institutional quality and regulatory frameworks [10], also play
                  a critical role in shaping innovation outcomes. Given the context-dependent nature of
                  innovation drivers, recent studies have focused on whether and how sustainability-ori-
                  ented practices, such as adopting GEPs, serve as an additional channel through which
                  firms enhance their innovation.
                     The Resource-Based View [19] posits that firms achieve competitive advantage by
                  developing resources and capabilities that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-sub-
                  stitutable. In particular, physical capital resources, including plant, machinery, equip-
                  ment, and production technology, constitute tangible assets that support firms’ strategic
                  activities. The adoption of GEPs typically entails targeted investments in such physical
                  capital, including energy-efficient machinery, cleaner production technologies, pollu-
                  tion-control equipment, and upgrades to logistics and transportation infrastructure.
                  These investments enhance the productivity and environmental efficiency of firms’
                  tangible assets while simultaneously embedding firm-specific technological capabili-
                  ties. As a result, the implementation of GEPs through physical capital upgrading can
                  strengthen firms’ resource bases and stimulate innovation by fostering the development
                  of new capabilities and operational processes [20], and facilitate compliance with regu-
                  latory and societal expectations [12]. Complementing the Resource-Based View, the
                  Porter Hypothesis [21] provides an innovation-oriented framework. It argues that well-
                  designed environmental practices and regulations can drive innovation by encouraging
                  firms to reduce inefficiencies, rethink production processes, and adopt cleaner technolo-
                  gies. From this perspective, adopting GEPs can generate innovation offsets that partially
Phan Discover Sustainability    (2026) 7:848                                                                     Page 4 of 17




                  or fully offset compliance costs [22], thereby reinforcing the positive link between the
                  adoption of GEPs and firm innovation.
                     Furthermore, Institutional Theory explains the link between GEPs and firm innovation
                  through the lens of institutional pressures and legitimacy. Firms often adopt GEPs in
                  response to coercive pressures (environmental regulations), normative pressures (indus-
                  try standards and professional norms), and mimetic pressures (the imitation of success-
                  ful peers). These drivers encourage firms to redesign internal processes and develop new
                  products. By conforming to these institutional expectations, firms enhance their legiti-
                  macy [23]; ultimately, this compliance-driven adaptation frequently catalyzes increased
                  innovation [24].
                     While extant literature establishes a positive correlation between sustainability initia-
                  tives and innovation outcomes, several critical gaps persist. Primarily, prior research
                  often relies on sustainability proxies, such as formal certifications, which reflect oper-
                  ational and managerial intent rather than the actual adoption of GEPs. For instance
                  [14], demonstrated that firms with CSR engagement, as evidenced by high CSR ratings,
                  possess superior innovative capabilities and higher new product introduction rates.
                  Similarly [15], observed that ISO 14000 certification, denoting the implementation of
                  environmental management systems, significantly augments both innovation inputs and
                  outputs. While these findings suggest that voluntary environmental frameworks can
                  catalyze innovation performance, the reliance on such certifications may overlook the
                  nuances of actual green integration.
                     Second, previous studies have primarily assessed the impact of green practice imple-
                  mentation indirectly through a regulatory lens, leading to inconsistent empirical evi-
                  dence. For example [25], utilized a quasi-natural experiment involving China’s National
                  Specially Monitored Firms program to demonstrate that heightened environmental
                  supervision stimulates innovation, particularly within state-owned enterprises, export-
                  ers, and large-scale firms. In contrast [26], found that China’s Carbon Intensity Con-
                  straint Policy actually suppressed corporate innovation. This inconsistency in prior
                  findings suggests that, although environmental oversight can act as a catalyst for techno-
                  logical progress, rigid regulatory mandates may weaken firms’ incentives to innovate by
                  imposing substantial resource constraints.
                     Third, a significant body of research focuses on domain-specific practices and their
                  respective innovation implications, particularly within energy management, emissions
                  control, and sustainable logistics. Regarding energy efficiency [27], found that firms
                  adopting climate-friendly management practices engage in more intensive climate-
                  related research and development, which facilitates long-term improvements in energy
                  performance. This finding is complemented by the work of [28], who utilized factor anal-
                  ysis and logistic regression on a sample of Danish manufacturing firms. They argue that
                  product innovation and energy efficiency are endogenously determined, suggesting that
                  firms may face strategic trade-offs when simultaneously optimizing these objectives.
                     Closely related to our study, recent empirical evidence examines the nexus between
                  the adoption of green-oriented practices and firm innovation, suggesting that such inte-
                  gration yields significant positive synergies and innovation-related gains. For instance
                  [29], employed partial least squares structural equation modeling to analyze survey data
                  from 3,184 firms. The findings demonstrated that pro-environmental practices are posi-
                  tively associated with green process, product, and organizational innovation. Similarly
Phan Discover Sustainability    (2026) 7:848                                                                    Page 5 of 17




                  [30], argued that adopting green practices supports firm innovation, particularly when
                  they foster the development of specialized sustainability-oriented capabilities. This
                  empirical evidence supports the perspective that sustainability serves as a transforma-
                  tive catalyst, driving the emergence of novel products and processes that challenge con-
                  ventional industrial paradigms [31].
                    Based on existing theories and empirical evidence, we propose the following
                  hypothesis:

                  H1: Firms with greater adoption of GEPs are more likely to engage in innovation activities.

                  3 The model and sample
                  3.1 The sample
                  Our dataset is drawn from the WBES, which provides firm-level data from a represen-
                  tative sample of manufacturing and service firms across more than 150 countries. The
                  standardized survey, initiated in 2006, is designed to capture a wide range of firm char-
                  acteristics and indicators of the business environment. In this study, we utilize the Green
                  Economy module, which collects data on firms’ perceptions and practices related to the
                  green economy in selected countries between 2018 and 2023. The original dataset covers
                  46 countries; however, four countries, namely Timor-Leste, Indonesia, Peru, and Bangla-
                  desh, were excluded due to non-standardized questions in this module. One limitation
                  of the WBES is the absence of a consistent panel structure across countries and survey
                  waves, which limits longitudinal analysis and the use of panel-specific econometric tech-
                  niques. Nevertheless, the WBES has been widely employed in recent corporate finance
                  and innovation studies [32–34]. After excluding missing observations, the final sample
                  comprises 18,860 firm-level observations from 42 countries over the 2018–2023 period.

                  3.2.1 Dependent variables
                  Firm innovation is proxied by two binary indicators: product innovation (PRODUCT)
                  and process innovation (PROCESS). PRODUCT is derived from the survey ques-
                  tion asking whether the firm introduced a new product or service. PROCESS captures
                  whether the firm introduced a new or significantly improved process.

                  3.2.2 Independent variable
                  We construct a GEP index as in [35] to measure the extent of firms’ engagement in GEPs.
                  The index is based on responses to a series of questions in the Green Economy module
                  of the WBES that ask whether the firm has adopted specific measures to reduce its envi-
                  ronmental impact. Ten measurements are requested, namely: (1) Heating and cooling
Phan Discover Sustainability     (2026) 7:848                                                                     Page 6 of 17




                  improvements; (2) Climate-friendly energy generation on site; (3) Machinery upgrades;
                  (4) Energy management systems; (5) Waste minimization, recycling, and waste manage-
                  ment; (6) Air pollution control measures; (7) Water management measures; (8) Upgrades
                  of vehicles, vessels, or aircraft in the fleet; (9) Improvement of lighting systems; and (10)
                  Other pollution control measures. Each adopted practice is assigned a score of 1, and the
                  index is calculated as the sum of the scores. The maximum possible score is ten, indicat-
                  ing that the firm has implemented all ten measures.

                  3.2.3 Control variables
                  The other firm-level control variables include cash flow (CFL), debt (DEBT), and firm
                  size (SIZE). We also account for ownership structure through foreign ownership (FO)
                  and the presence of large shareholders (LARGE). Additionally, we control for firm age
                  (AGE) and human capital, proxied by the proportion of skilled workers (SKILL). Table 1
                  provides detailed descriptions of all variables and their corresponding survey question
                  codes.

                  3.3 Methodology
                  We employ logistic regression to examine how the adoption of GEPs influences firm
                  innovation. Logistic regression is an appropriate method when the dependent variable is
                  binary. This model estimates the probability of a firm engaging in innovation as a func-
                  tion of a set of explanatory variables. The predicted values from the logistic regression
                  range between 0 and 1, representing the estimated likelihood of the outcome. In this
                  study, the dependent variable is a binary indicator equal to 1 if the firm engages in inno-
                  vation activities and 0 otherwise. While the raw logistic coefficients represent changes in
                  the log-odds, they are often exponentiated and interpreted as odds ratios: a value greater
                  than 1 indicates that an increase in the independent variable (adopting more GEPs) is
                  associated with higher odds of the firm engaging in innovation. In contrast, a value less
                  than 1 suggests a negative association.
                    We first estimate a baseline model using the composite GEP index to capture the
                  overall intensity of GEP adoption. We then estimate separate regressions for each GEP
                  dimension to examine heterogeneity in the relationship between specific green practices
                  and firm innovation outcomes. Estimating each dimension separately allows us to assess
                  the association between a given practice and innovation without imposing a joint struc-
                  ture across potentially highly correlated practices. This approach mitigates concerns
                  about multicollinearity and facilitates clearer interpretation of the effects of individual

                  4.2 Results and discussion
                  4.2.1 Baseline results
                  Table 6 reports the odds ratios from logistic regression models examining the relation-
                  ship between the GEP index and firm innovation outcomes. The estimates indicate that
                  the GEP index is positively and significantly associated with innovation. Specifically, a
                  one-unit increase in the GEP index, representing the adoption of one additional GEP, is
                  associated with significantly higher odds of both product innovation (odds ratio = 1.157,
                  p < 0.01) and process innovation (odds ratio = 1.194, p < 0.01). In percentage terms, the
                  adoption of an additional GEP is associated with an increase of 15.7% and 19.4% in the
                  odds of product and process innovation, respectively. Several underlying mechanisms
                  can explain the positive relationship. The adoption of GEPs encourages firms to use
                  energy, materials, and water more efficiently, prompting them to redesign production
                  systems, implement cleaner technologies, and optimize resource flows to reduce waste
                  and emissions [36, 37]. At the same time, firms may develop new eco-efficient products

                  5 Conclusion and implications
                  This study investigates the impact of GEP adoption on firm-level product and process
                  innovation. Using a WBES dataset of 18,860 observations, a composite GEP index was
                  constructed using data from the Green Economy module. The logistic regression results
                  indicate that firms that implement more green practices are more likely to engage in
                  both product and process innovation. Further analysis of the ten individual dimensions
                  of GEPs, including (1) heating and cooling improvements; (2) climate-friendly on-site
Phan Discover Sustainability    (2026) 7:848                                                                     Page 14 of 17




                  energy generation; (3) machinery upgrades; (4) energy management systems; (5) waste
                  minimization, recycling, and management; (6) air pollution control; (7) water manage-
                  ment; (8) fleet upgrades (vehicles, vessels, or aircraft); (9) lighting system improvements;
                  and (10) other pollution control measures, reveals that each dimension is positively
                  associated with firm innovation. Based on these results, several implications are pro-
                  posed below.

                  5.1 Theoretical implications
                  This study contributes to the literature by linking environmental management and inno-
                  vation from three theoretical perspectives: Institutional Theory, the Resource-Based
                  View, and the Porter Hypothesis. First, the findings provide empirical support for Insti-
                  tutional Theory. Previous studies often suggest that firms may adopt green practices as
                  symbolic actions to satisfy regulators or external stakeholders. However, our results indi-
                  cate that firms respond to regulatory and social pressures through substantive organiza-
                  tional changes that make them more likely to engage in innovation. This result suggests
                  that conformity to environmental expectations does not necessarily weaken competi-
                  tiveness. Instead, complying with green standards can serve as a foundation for improv-
                  ing internal processes and developing new capabilities. In this way, the study offers a
                  more nuanced understanding of how institutional pressures can contribute to firm-level
                  innovation.
                     Second, our results extend the Resource-Based View by showing that GEPs are more
                  than just operational costs; they are a source of valuable organizational capabilities.
                  While the initial investment often involves physical capital, such as machinery and
                  energy systems, the real value lies in the technical knowledge and improved routines that
                  firms develop during the implementation process. These firm-specific strengths may be
                  difficult for competitors to replicate. Our study confirms that these practices should be
                  viewed as strategic resources that enhance efficiency and directly support long-term
                  product and process innovation.
                     Third, the findings provide support for the strong version of the Porter Hypothesis. By
                  examining a wide range of green practices, we show that environmental initiatives can
                  lead to innovation offsets. Instead of being a permanent financial drain, activities like
                  redesigning processes or upgrading technology help firms become more efficient over
                  time. Our study demonstrates that environmental responsibility is not a burden; rather,
                  it acts as a driver for long-term competitiveness. This shifts the focus away from a win-
                  lose trade-off and toward a win-win scenario, where green standards encourage, rather
                  than hinder, technological creativity.

                  5.2 Managerial and policy implications
                  5.2.1 Managerial implications
                  The findings of this study offer several important implications for managers seek-
                  ing to enhance innovation through environmental initiatives. First, the positive asso-
                  ciation between GEP adoption and both product and process innovation suggests that
                  green practices should not be viewed merely as regulatory compliance or cost burdens.
                  Instead, managers can approach environmental initiatives as strategic investments that
                  stimulate technological upgrading. Firms that systematically adopt a broader portfolio of
Phan Discover Sustainability    (2026) 7:848                                                                    Page 15 of 17




                  green practices are more likely to enhance their innovative capacity, indicating that envi-
                  ronmental strategy can serve as a catalyst for competitive differentiation.
                    Second, the results show that each of the ten individual dimensions of GEPs is posi-
                  tively linked to innovation outcomes. This implies that managers do not need to rely on
                  a single flagship initiative to generate innovative benefits. Operational improvements,
                  such as heating and cooling upgrades, lighting system modernization, waste manage-
                  ment systems, or energy management tools, can individually contribute to innovation.
                  Therefore, managers may pursue incremental and modular green investments that fit
                  their resource constraints while still generating innovation-enhancing effects.

                  5.2.2 Policy implications
                  The findings of this study also suggest several important implications for policymakers
                  aiming to promote sustainable development and innovation-led growth. First, the posi-
                  tive relationship between GEP adoption and both product and process innovation sug-
                  gests that environmental policies can yield dual benefits: environmental improvement
                  and technological advancement. Rather than imposing excessive compliance burdens,
                  well-designed environmental regulations may stimulate firms to modernize production
                  systems and introduce innovative products. Policymakers should therefore view green
                  regulations not only as environmental protection instruments but also as mechanisms to
                  enhance national innovation capacity.
                    Second, because the adoption of multiple green practices appears to strengthen inno-
                  vation outcomes, policymakers should design integrated policy frameworks rather than
                  fragmented initiatives. Coordinated measures, combining environmental standards,
                  green financing schemes, R&D subsidies, and technical advisory programs, can encour-
                  age firms to adopt complementary green practices and build long-term capabilities.
                  Such systemic support is particularly important for small and medium-sized enterprises
                  that may face resource constraints.

                  5.3 Limitations and future research
                  Despite the significant insights, this study is subject to several limitations that pave
                  the way for future research. First, while the GEP index and its ten dimensions cap-
                  ture various aspects of green practices, they may not fully reflect the intensity, quality,
                  or effectiveness of implementation within firms. The current measures primarily indi-
                  cate whether specific practices have been adopted, but they do not capture the depth of
                  integration or the level of managerial commitment underlying these initiatives. Future
                  research could rely on in-depth interviews or case studies to examine how firms inter-
                  nally execute GEPs, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of capability
                  development.
                    Second, this study relies on cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to observe
                  how the relationship between GEPs and innovation evolves over time. Because green
                  investments and innovation outcomes rarely occur simultaneously, establishing a clear
                  cause-and-effect relationship remains challenging. Future research should prioritize
                  longitudinal designs to track the long-term development of green capabilities. Such
                  approaches would help identify potential time-lag effects and clarify the period between
                  the initial adoption of green practices and the realization of innovation benefits.

                    Third, while the cross-country evidence provides a broad overview, it may not fully
                  capture how national institutional environments influence the relationship between
                  GEPs and innovation. National programs, such as carbon pricing mechanisms or green
                  R&D grants, vary significantly across countries and can act as important moderating fac-
                  tors. Future research should examine these country-specific policies to determine how
                  the regulatory framework in a firm’s home country strengthens or weakens the impact
                  of GEPs on innovation outcomes. This would provide a more detailed understanding of
                  how government support interacts with firm-level green initiatives.
