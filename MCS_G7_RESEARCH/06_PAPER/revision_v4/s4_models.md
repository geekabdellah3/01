# 4. Formalising management control frameworks and mapping them to WBES

**Frameworks formalised.** Table 1 states, in a common notation, the frameworks used in the literature on management control. The specifications are restricted to what bibliographic records support; where a record gave only the number of elements (for example the twelve questions of Ferreira and Otley, 2009), the elements are not itemised. Malmi and Brown (2008) treat controls as a package of five types, so that the package is the joint distribution of control-type constructs and a configuration is a region of that space. Simons (1995) and Tessier and Otley (2012) distinguish four systems and modes of use, so that effects depend on use rather than presence. Otley (1999) and Ferreira and Otley (2009) describe areas of a performance management system (objectives, strategies and plans, targets, rewards, information flows); Merchant and Van der Stede distinguish results, action, personnel and cultural controls; Ouchi (1979) distinguishes market, bureaucratic and clan mechanisms; Grabner and Moers (2013) define a system as a package with internal consistency; Bloom and Van Reenen (2007) summarise practices in a score.

**Mapping and coverage.** Each construct was mapped to WBES items with a strength (strong, proxy, none) that reflects both conceptual fit and the verification status of the item wording (Table 2, Figure 2); the coverage score (observed + 0.5 × partial, divided by constructs) is a summary of that judgement, not a measurement. The scoring-based tradition has full coverage by construction. Frameworks that centre on targets, rewards and information flows are covered in part, the package of Malmi and Brown is covered only for cybernetic and reward controls, and the constructs that distinguish the control literature from the management-practice literature — belief and boundary systems, interactive use, administrative and cultural controls, clan control and internal consistency — are not observed.

**What this implies.** (i) WBES can address the cybernetic, results and reward controls and the organisational contingencies; it cannot address use, integration or consistency. (ii) Competing frameworks differ mostly in constructs that WBES does not observe, so the data can compare their implications only for the observed subset. (iii) Claims about the structure of a control *system* cannot be derived from the data. Section 7.10 tests whether the observed items follow the item structures implied by the frameworks.
