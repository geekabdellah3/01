# 1. Introduction

Monitoring, target setting and incentives are the structured management practices that Bloom and Van Reenen (2007) use to measure management and relate it to productivity, and that later work treats as a transferable managerial technology (Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen, 2016). They also correspond to core mechanisms of management control: measuring results, setting objectives and rewarding performance. This article starts from these three observable practices and asks whether they show the same individual and joint associations with performance in seven advanced economies, and how far a model estimated in one country, or in several, transfers to another.

The reasoning proceeds in two steps. First, the practices are measured as the World Bank Enterprise Surveys (WBES) record them and related to performance separately, in combination and across contexts. Second, the control literature (Malmi & Brown, 2008; Grabner & Moers, 2013; Simons, 1995) and the general management literature on contingency and institutions (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Kostova, 1999) are used to interpret what the associations can and cannot mean. The WBES items are therefore not treated as an incomplete questionnaire for any particular control framework; they are the observable practices from which propositions of those frameworks are put to the test.

The proposition examined is the following: although structured management practices are commonly conceptualised as transferable managerial technologies, their relationship with organisational performance may depend not only on their individual adoption but also on how monitoring, target-setting and incentive mechanisms are combined across organisational and national contexts. It is formulated as a proposition to test, not as a result. Three levels of analysis follow: (1) the association of each practice with performance, (2) the combinations of practices, and (3) the stability and transferability of these associations across organisational and national contexts.

The evidence is cross-sectional, concerns reported practices, and cannot show whether practices are integrated into decision-making or how they are used; mechanisms of integration and use are therefore treated as theoretical explanations to be tested with other data.
