# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 Interpretation through general management frameworks

Table 8 states what each framework implies for the data and how the evidence reads.

**Management as a technology (partly supported).** Practices share a dominant continuous dimension in every country (partial invariance), adoption differs widely across countries, and the association with productivity is positive in five of seven countries. Heterogeneity in slopes is detectable in most specifications, and the association is weak in France and the United Kingdom, so a strictly universal effect is not supported.

**Contingency and capabilities (not supported with available contingencies).** None of the measured contingencies — size, age, foreign ownership, exports, manager experience or gender — moderates the association reliably after correction. This does not refute contingency theory: the contingencies that theory emphasises, such as environmental uncertainty, technology and strategy, are not measured.

**Institutional decoupling and transfer (consistent, not discriminated).** The countries with the highest reported adoption have the weakest slopes, and the association flattens in the upper third of the score. This is what decoupling or ceremonial adoption would produce (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Kostova & Roth, 2002), but it is equally what a ceiling effect, restricted range or measurement error would produce: reliability is lowest in the same countries, and a common level-dependent shape absorbs part of the cross-country heterogeneity. A measure of integration or use would be required to separate these explanations. The suggestive moderation by foreign ownership is compatible with a transfer reading but is not a test of it.

**Equifinality (inconclusive).** Combinations of practices with equal core score are neither shown to be equivalent nor shown to differ reliably.

**Universality of practices, structure, effectiveness and fit.** Practices are widely observed; structure is largely common; effectiveness is positive but heterogeneous and level-dependent; contextual fit is not identified. Seven countries cannot separate institutions, culture, sampling frames and sector composition, and country effects are not measures of any of them.

## 7.2 Rapprochement with the management control literature

Table 9 relates the general concepts to their management control counterparts. Adoption of formal practices corresponds to the presence of controls in a package (Malmi & Brown, 2008) and to the institutionalisation of rules and routines (Burns & Scapens, 2000); the observed common dimension therefore describes an accumulation of cybernetic and reward controls, not a designed system. Decoupling corresponds to the distinction between package and system (Grabner & Moers, 2013) and between presence and use of controls (Simons, 1995; Tessier & Otley, 2012): a firm may report indicators, targets and bonuses that do not operate as a coherent system. The weak association at high adoption is consistent with this reading but does not demonstrate it. The null firm-level moderation is consistent with Otley's (2016) argument that questionnaire-based contingency research is too narrow to identify mechanisms. Cross-country heterogeneity that cannot be traced to measured institutions illustrates the difficulty of cross-cultural control research (Harrison & McKinnon, 1999) and the risk of culture-as-explanation (McSweeney, 2002). Combinations of practices at equal sophistication offer no evidence of distinct configurations (cf. Bedford & Malmi, 2015), but only a subset of controls is observed.

## 7.3 What cannot be concluded
That the same control arrangements are equally appropriate everywhere, or that each context requires its own system, is not established. Integration, use and context are unmeasured, and all results are cross-sectional associations.
