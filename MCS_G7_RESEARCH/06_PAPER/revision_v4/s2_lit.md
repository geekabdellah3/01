## 2.1 General management frameworks

**Management as a technology.** Bloom and Van Reenen (2007, 2010) measure practices of monitoring, target setting and incentives and relate them to productivity; differences in practices persist because of weak competition, family ownership, regulation and informational barriers. Bloom, Sadun and Van Reenen (2016) ask whether practices are akin to a technology or contingent styles and report large cross-country differences in adoption. A universalist reading predicts a common structure and a stable positive association with performance, with countries differing mainly in adoption.

**Contingency and fit.** Contingency theory (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Donaldson, 2001) holds that no single structure is best and that performance depends on fit with contingencies such as size, environment and technology. Drazin and Van de Ven (1985) distinguish selection, interaction and systems forms of fit; Gresov and Drazin (1997) distinguish forms of equifinality. A contingency reading predicts that the practice–performance association varies with measured contingencies.

**Institutional theory and decoupling.** Formal structures may reflect institutionalised rules and be decoupled from operations (Meyer & Rowan, 1977), isomorphic pressures make organisations similar (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), and rhetoric about a practice can diverge from its use (Zbaracki, 1998). Varieties-of-capitalism work stresses national institutional complementarities (Hall & Soskice, 2001). An institutional reading predicts widespread adoption with a weaker link to outcomes where adoption is driven by legitimacy rather than use.

**Transfer of practices and capabilities.** Kostova (1999) defines transfer success as institutionalisation of the practice at the recipient unit and models it as shaped by country institutional profile, organisational context and individuals; Kostova and Roth (2002) identify conditions of ceremonial adoption. Absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) and dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2007) suggest that implementation depends on prior knowledge and capabilities.

**Culture, institutions and country effects.** Culture, institutions and country fixed effects are different things; McSweeney (2002) criticises treating national cultures as causal entities. The data contain only country identifiers, which cannot be read as measures of culture or institutions.

**Exploratory methods.** Nielsen (2022) argues for exploratory data analysis and unsupervised learning in management accounting research. We use latent class and continuous latent-variable models as complementary lenses; statistical structure is not theoretical interpretation, and neither method guarantees that the structure found corresponds to meaningful organisational systems.

## 2.3 Relating the framework to management control (second step)
The management control literature supplies domain-specific counterparts of these general concepts. Malmi and Brown (2008) propose a package of planning, cybernetic, reward, administrative and cultural controls; Grabner and Moers (2013) distinguish packages from systems with internal consistency, which parallels the distinction between adoption and integration; Simons (1995) and Tessier and Otley (2012) distinguish uses of controls, which parallels the distinction between adoption and implementation; Burns and Scapens (2000) treat management accounting change as institutionalisation of rules and routines; Chenhall (2003) and Otley (2016) review contingency research on control; Harrison and McKinnon (1999) review cross-cultural control research; Bedford and Malmi (2015) study configurations of control.
