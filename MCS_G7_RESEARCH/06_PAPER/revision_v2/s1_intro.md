# 1. Introduction

Management control practices such as performance indicators, formal targets and performance-related pay are promoted internationally as good practice. A long-standing question in management accounting is whether such practices operate in the same way wherever they are adopted, or whether their effectiveness depends on their fit with organisational and institutional conditions (Otley, 2016; Chenhall, 2003). Two research traditions point in different directions. Economists measuring management practices report that more structured management is associated with higher productivity across firms and countries (Bloom & Van Reenen, 2007), a largely universalist reading. Management-control scholars stress that controls operate as a package (Malmi & Brown, 2008) whose elements must be integrated into a system (Grabner & Moers, 2013), and that effects depend on how controls are used (Simons, 1995) and on context.

This article asks to what extent the structure and the performance associations of observable management control practices are universal across advanced economies, and to what extent they are contingent on national and organisational contexts. We use the management-practices module of the World Bank Enterprise Surveys (WBES) for seven G7 economies in 2024–2025 (6,316 establishments). We separate four ideas that are often conflated: universality of *practices* (they are observed everywhere), of *structure* (they share a common statistical dimension), of *effectiveness* (their association with performance is stable) and *contextual fit* (effectiveness depends on alignment with context). Only the first three can be examined with WBES; contextual fit can be examined only indirectly and is treated as a theoretical interpretation.

The paper preserves the findings of an earlier exploratory phase, in which observed practices were found to be organised predominantly along a continuous sophistication dimension rather than in distinct latent classes, and adds new analyses of measurement comparability, cross-country heterogeneity in the practice–performance association, firm-level contingencies and equifinality. Its contribution is bounded: it shows what co-occurrence data can and cannot say about control systems, and it formulates, without claiming to test, a proposition about the coexistence of a common architecture of practices with heterogeneous outcomes.
